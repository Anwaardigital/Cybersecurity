How to find out about breaches

  • Reputable breach-notification services (e.g. Have I Been Pwned) let you check if your email appears in known breach datasets. Use well-known services only.
  • Many password managers and browsers now warn you automatically when a saved login appears in a breach — turn those alerts on.
  • Companies must notify affected users under laws like GDPR — don't ignore those emails (but verify them: navigate to the site yourself rather than clicking).

Make the next breach boring

  • Unique password per site (password manager) — one breach can't cascade.
  • 2FA everywhere — stolen passwords alone become useless.
  • Share less: every optional field you fill is future breach fuel. Skip what isn't required.
  • Delete old accounts you no longer use — fewer copies of your data, fewer targets.

About This Tool

What this tool does

The Data Breach Response Guide tells you what to do when your personal information has been exposed in a breach — for example, when a company emails you saying attackers accessed customer data, or when a breach-checking service shows your email in a leaked database.

It organizes your response by priority: securing the accounts most at risk first, then working outward to everything that shared a password or recovery email with the breached service. The guide also explains what the breached company should be doing and what to watch for in the months after a breach, when stolen data is most often abused.

It is for anyone with online accounts. Breaches are common enough that responding calmly and correctly is now a basic life skill. The tone throughout is practical and defensive: protect yourself, document what happened, and use the event to strengthen your setup.

How to use it

  1. Confirm the breach is real. Check the company's official site or a reputable breach-notification service rather than trusting an alarming email alone.
  2. Change the password on the breached account immediately, from a device you trust, and enable 2FA if you have not already.
  3. Change the password on every other account where you reused the same or a similar password.
  4. Review the breached account for unauthorized activity: new email rules, changed recovery addresses, unknown orders or messages.
  5. Watch for follow-up phishing. Breach news triggers waves of fake "security alert" emails exploiting the same event.
  6. If financial data was involved, notify your bank, consider a credit freeze or fraud alert where available, and monitor statements closely.

Defensive security tips

  • Assume breach data lives forever. Leaked emails and passwords circulate for years. Unique passwords per site contain the damage to one account.
  • Treat breach news as phishing season. Attackers send fake breach notifications with malicious links within hours of real announcements. Navigate to sites manually.
  • Freeze or monitor credit where possible. If identity documents or financial data were exposed, proactive monitoring limits the damage from identity theft.
  • Keep a breach log. Note which service, what data was exposed, and what you changed. It helps you answer "was my password from that breach?" months later.
  • Use the breach as a prompt. Every breach is a good moment to adopt a password manager and 2FA if you have been postponing them.

FAQs

How do I know if my data was in a breach?

Reputable breach-notification services let you search your email address against known breach databases. Companies are also generally required to notify affected customers directly — verify such notices through the company's official channels.

I reused the breached password elsewhere. How urgent is this?

Very. Attackers automate "credential stuffing": trying stolen email-password pairs on hundreds of other sites within hours of a leak. Change those passwords immediately, starting with email and banking.

What if the breached company offers free credit monitoring?

Accept it — it is part of what they owe you — but do not rely on it alone. Monitoring detects misuse; unique passwords and 2FA prevent it.

Can I get my data removed from breach databases?

Generally no. Once data is copied and traded, it cannot be recalled. Your defense is making the stolen data useless: changed passwords, 2FA, and vigilance against phishing that uses the leaked details.
