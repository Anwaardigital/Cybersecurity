How to Detect and Remove Stalkerware from Your Phone (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 15 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 10 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Imagine this: someone always seems to know where you are, who you called, and what you typed — even though you never told them. That is not a coincidence. It may be stalkerware: hidden software installed on your phone that secretly watches everything you do and sends it to another person. Stalkerware is usually installed by someone who has physical access to your phone — a jealous partner, an ex, a controlling family member, or even an employer who went too far. It is a serious violation of privacy, and in many countries, including Pakistan, secretly monitoring another adult without their consent is illegal. This guide is written for the person whose phone may be watched: how to spot the warning signs, how to check your phone step by step, how to remove stalkerware safely, and how to protect yourself afterwards. Everything here is defensive. This guide will never tell you how to install monitoring software on someone else's phone — that is exactly the harm we are helping you stop.

What is stalkerware?

Stalkerware is a type of spyware designed to watch a specific person. Unlike ordinary malware, which usually wants your money or your passwords, stalkerware wants your life: your location, your calls, your messages, your photos, and even what you type. It is often sold openly as a "parental control" or "employee monitoring" app, but it is used to secretly track adults who never agreed to be tracked. Once installed, stalkerware usually hides itself: it may not appear in your app drawer, it may use a boring name like "System Update" or "Sync Service", and it runs quietly in the background, sending your data to a website or app that the other person controls. On Android, stalkerware often needs special permissions — access to your location, microphone, contacts, and messages — and it may ask to be made a "device administrator" so it cannot be uninstalled easily. On iPhone, true stalkerware is rarer because Apple's system is more locked down, but a person with your Apple ID password can still track you through iCloud, Find My, and shared accounts — so iPhone users need to check their accounts, not just their apps. Stalkerware is different from the legal parental controls you set up for your own young child, and different from a company phone that your employer openly told you is monitored. The key question is consent: if someone is watching your phone and you did not agree to it, that is stalkerware, and you have every right to remove it.

8 warning signs your phone may have stalkerware

No single sign proves stalkerware is present — a hot phone can just mean a hot day, and a draining battery can just mean an old battery. But several of these signs together should make you check your phone carefully:

  • Your battery drains much faster than usual. Stalkerware is always running in the background, recording and uploading your data. If your battery suddenly lasts half as long as it used to, with no new app or update to explain it, something hidden may be working overtime.
  • Your phone gets hot when you are not using it. A phone sitting idle on a table should stay cool. If it feels warm for no reason, a background app may be recording audio, tracking location, or uploading files.
  • Your mobile data usage jumps suddenly. Stalkerware sends your calls, messages, photos and location to someone else's server, and that uses data. Check your data usage in Settings — if an app you barely use has consumed hundreds of megabytes, investigate it.
  • Your phone restarts, slows down, or behaves strangely. Random restarts, apps crashing, the screen lighting up by itself, or strange noises during calls can all be signs that something is running in the background that should not be there.
  • Settings change by themselves. Your location is turned on when you turned it off. Unknown Wi-Fi networks appear. Your screen lock is disabled, or a new security setting appears. Stalkerware needs these settings to keep watching you, so it changes them back.
  • You see apps you do not recognise. An app with a generic name — "System Service", "Device Health", "Update Manager" — that you never installed is the most direct warning sign of all. Check your full app list, not just your home screen.
  • Someone knows things they should not know. This is the most important sign of all. If a partner, ex, or family member quotes your private messages, knows exactly where you went, or mentions a call you made in private, trust your instinct: they may be reading your phone, not guessing.
  • Your Google or Apple account shows unknown activity. A new device signed in to your account, a location history you do not recognise, or a "your account was accessed from a new device" email can mean someone is tracking you through your account rather than through an app on your phone.

If you recognise several of these signs, do not panic — work through the checks below one by one. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the same warning signs in more detail, and our malware removal guide explains the general clean-up steps that also apply here.

Check 1: Look for unknown apps

Start with the simplest check. On Android, open Settings > Apps > See all apps and scroll slowly through the complete list — not just your home screen, where stalkerware never appears. Look for apps with generic or technical-sounding names you do not remember installing: "System Update", "Sync Service", "Device Health", "Wi-Fi Service", or anything with no icon. Tap any suspicious app and look at its details: when was it installed, how much data has it used, and what permissions does it hold? A "system" app installed last week that has used 500 MB of data is not a system app. On iPhone, open Settings > General > iPhone Storage and review the full app list the same way. Also check for configuration profiles, which are a common way to control an iPhone remotely: go to Settings > General > VPN & Device Management. If you see a profile you did not install, that is a serious red flag — do not delete it yet if you may need evidence (see the safety section below), but make a note of its name.

Check 2: Review app permissions

Stalkerware needs powerful permissions to do its job, so your permission lists are a good place to hunt. On Android, open Settings > Privacy > Permission manager and check the most sensitive permissions one by one: Location, Microphone, Camera, SMS, Contacts, and Call Logs. For each permission, ask: does this app need it? Your maps app needs location; a calculator does not. A flashlight app with access to your microphone and messages is not a flashlight app. Pay special attention to apps that have "Allow all the time" location access — very few apps genuinely need to track your location when you are not using them. On iPhone, open Settings > Privacy & Security and review Location Services, Microphone, Camera, and Contacts the same way. Remove any permission that makes no sense, and remove it from the app entirely if the app itself looks suspicious. While you are there, check Settings > Privacy > Location Services > System Services > Significant Locations on iPhone, or Google Maps > Timeline on Android, to see whether your location history is being recorded — and whether anyone else could be viewing it.

Check 3: Check device administrator apps

On Android, stalkerware often asks to become a "device administrator" — a special status that stops you from uninstalling it normally. To check, open Settings > Security > Device admin apps (on some phones this is under Settings > Security > More security settings). You should normally see only Find My Device and perhaps your work profile. If you see an unknown app listed as a device administrator, that is a strong sign of stalkerware. Do not switch it off immediately if you think you may need evidence for the police — but do make a note of the app's name, and know that this is the switch you will use when you are ready to remove it. On iPhone there is no direct equivalent, but check Settings > General > VPN & Device Management for profiles and Settings > [Your Name] > Find My to see who your location is being shared with, and check whether your Apple ID is signed in on a device you do not own.

Check 4: Check battery and data usage

Your phone keeps a record of which apps use the most battery and data — and stalkerware cannot hide from this record. On Android, open Settings > Battery > Battery usage and look at the last 24 hours. On iPhone, open Settings > Battery and scroll down to the per-app list. An app you rarely open should not be near the top of the list. Then check data: on Android, open Settings > Network & internet > Data usage and look at mobile data per app; on iPhone, open Settings > Mobile Data and scroll through the list. If an unknown or rarely used app has consumed a large amount of data, that app is uploading something — and you should find out what. Take screenshots of anything suspicious before you change it: these records disappear when the app is removed, and they can be useful evidence later.

Check 5: Run a scan with Google Play Protect and a security app

Automated scans will not catch every stalkerware app — some are sold as "legitimate" monitoring tools, so they are not flagged as malware — but they catch many of them, and the check takes only a minute. On Android, open the Play Store, tap your profile picture, tap Play Protect, and tap Scan. Google Play Protect also has a specific stalkerware warning: if it finds an app that can track you, it will warn you directly. You can also install a trusted mobile security app from a well-known company and run a full scan — our mobile security guide explains how to choose one and what to look for. On iPhone, there is no antivirus scan in the same sense, because apps are sandboxed — instead, run Apple's own check: open Settings > Privacy & Security > Safety Check. Safety Check is built for exactly this situation: it shows you who can see your location and access your data, which apps have sensitive permissions, and lets you quickly stop sharing with everyone in an emergency. If Safety Check shows that your location or data is shared with someone you did not intend, you have found your answer.

You found stalkerware — what to do now (safety first)

Finding stalkerware is frightening, and your first instinct may be to delete it immediately. Sometimes that is the right move — but not always. If the person watching you is violent or controlling, removing the app suddenly can put you in danger, because they will know you found it. Read this section carefully before you touch anything:

  1. Put your safety first, not the phone. If you are in an abusive relationship or you feel unsafe, your safety matters more than the phone. Contact a trusted person, a local support organisation, or the police before you remove anything. In Pakistan, you can report cyber harassment to the FIA Cybercrime Wing — our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps, the evidence to keep, and how to file a complaint.
  2. Do not use the monitored phone to ask for help. If someone is reading your messages and tracking your location, do not plan your escape on that phone. Borrow a trusted friend's phone, use a public computer, or go to a support centre in person. Your messages, calls, and location on the watched phone may all be visible to the person monitoring you.
  3. Decide whether you need evidence. If you plan to report the person to the police, the stalkerware itself is evidence — and deleting the app destroys it. Before you delete anything, take screenshots of the suspicious app, its permissions, its battery and data usage, and its device-admin status. Save the screenshots somewhere safe, such as a trusted friend's phone or a new email account the other person does not know about. If you are unsure, ask the police or a support organisation first — they can advise you on what to preserve.
  4. Remove the app safely. When you are ready, first switch off its device-administrator status (Android: Settings > Security > Device admin apps), then uninstall it from Settings > Apps. If the app refuses to uninstall, or if it comes back after you remove it, the phone may need a factory reset — the strongest clean-up available. Before a factory reset, back up your photos and contacts to a safe place (a computer or a cloud account the other person cannot access), then reset the phone and set it up as a new device, not from a backup, so the stalkerware does not come back with it. Our back-up guide explains how to save your data safely first.
  5. Change your passwords from a safe device. After the phone is clean, change the passwords for your Google or Apple account, your email, and your banking apps — from a different, trusted device, not from the phone you just cleaned. Turn on two-factor authentication with an authenticator app, and check your account's signed-in devices and remove any you do not recognise. Our two-factor authentication guide and our password manager guide walk you through this step by step.
  6. Check your other accounts and devices. Stalkerware is often installed alongside other abuse: the same person may know your email password, have your location shared through Find My, or have installed something on your laptop too. Check your Google account's security page and your Apple ID device list, review who your location is shared with, and run a scan on your computer as well. Our malware removal guide covers the computer side.

How to prevent stalkerware

Once your phone is clean, these habits make it very hard for anyone to install stalkerware again:

  • Lock your phone with a strong PIN or password — not just a fingerprint. Stalkerware is almost always installed by someone who physically holds your phone for a few minutes. A strong lock screen is your first defence. Never share your PIN or password with a partner, and change it immediately if you think someone has seen it.
  • Never leave your phone unlocked and unattended. It takes only a few minutes to install a hidden tracking app. Keep your phone with you, set it to lock after 30 seconds, and do not hand it to someone to "make a call" if you do not fully trust them.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere. Even if someone knows your password, two-factor authentication stops them signing in to your Google or Apple account, where they could track you through Find My, read your backed-up messages, or see your location history.
  • Keep your phone's software updated. Updates fix the security holes that spyware uses to hide and to stay installed. Turn on automatic updates for your operating system and your apps.
  • Install apps only from the official Play Store or App Store. Never install an APK file sent in a message, and never let anyone "sideload" an app onto your phone. Our fake apps guide explains how to check any app before installing it.
  • Review your permissions and signed-in devices regularly. Once a month, spend five minutes checking your app permissions, your device-admin apps, and the devices signed in to your Google or Apple account. A quick monthly check catches a problem early, before it becomes a crisis.
  • Do not share your cloud account. Sharing a Google or Apple account with a partner means they can see your photos, your location, and your messages without installing anything at all. Keep your accounts separate, and share only specific things — like a single photo album — never the whole account.

Key Takeaways

  • Stalkerware is hidden software that watches your calls, messages, photos and location — usually installed by someone who physically held your phone for a few minutes.
  • Watch for the warning signs: fast battery drain, a hot phone, sudden data use, changed settings, unknown apps, and — most importantly — someone knowing things they should not know.
  • Check your phone step by step: unknown apps, app permissions, device-admin apps, battery and data usage, and a Play Protect or Safety Check scan.
  • Safety comes before deletion. If the person watching you may be dangerous, get help first, do not plan on the monitored phone, and preserve evidence before you remove the app.
  • After removal, change your passwords from a safe device, turn on two-factor authentication, and check your other accounts and devices too.
  • Prevention is simple: a strong lock screen, never sharing your PIN, automatic updates, official app stores only, and a monthly permission check.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your own phone and your own privacy, with no attempt to monitor anyone else.
