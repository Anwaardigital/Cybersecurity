How to Tell If Your Phone Is Hacked (And What to Do Next) (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 12 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 11 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Your phone knows almost everything about you — your bank, your photos, your chats, your location and your contacts. That is exactly why attackers want into it. The good news is that a hacked phone almost always leaves clues: the battery drains too fast, apps appear that you never installed, settings change by themselves, or your friends receive strange messages from your number. The bad news is that most people ignore these signs for weeks, and every extra day gives an attacker more time to read messages, steal codes and empty accounts. This guide shows you the 12 warning signs that your phone may be hacked, how to tell a real attack apart from an innocent glitch, and the exact steps to clean your Android phone or iPhone and lock it down again. Everything here is defensive: it is about protecting your own phone, never about accessing anyone else's.

How do phones get hacked in the first place?

Phones are rarely hacked by a stranger typing code into a dark room. In real life, the attack almost always starts with something small and ordinary:

  • A fake app. You install a free app from outside the Play Store or App Store, or a lookalike app that copies a popular one. Hidden inside it is malware that watches what you type or shows ads and steals data.
  • A phishing link. A text, WhatsApp message or email pretends to be your bank, a courier company or a prize offer. You tap the link, enter your password or download a file, and the attacker is in.
  • Public Wi-Fi or a fake charger. An unsecured network or a compromised public USB charging point can be used to push malicious software onto a phone. Our public Wi-Fi safety guide explains this risk in detail.
  • An old, unpatched phone. Every software update fixes security holes. A phone that has not been updated in a year is an easy target, because the holes in it are publicly known.
  • A weak password or no lock screen. If someone gets your phone in their hands for even a minute — a borrowed phone, a phone left on a table — they can install spyware or change your account settings.

Knowing how it happens matters, because the fix is not only cleaning the phone — it is also closing the door the attacker came through, so it does not happen again.

12 warning signs your phone may be hacked

One sign on its own usually means nothing. Two or three together, especially if they started at the same time, mean you should investigate today, not next week.

1. The battery drains far too fast

Malware runs in the background all day — recording, uploading, sending data — and that uses power. If a fully charged phone that used to last a full day now dies by the afternoon, and you have not changed how you use it, check Settings → Battery to see which app is using the most power. An app you barely use sitting at the top of that list is a red flag.

2. The phone gets hot when you are not using it

A phone warms up during a long video call or a game — that is normal. A phone that feels hot while sitting untouched on a table is not. Background malware, mining software or a runaway app can keep the processor working non-stop. Feel the back of the phone after it has been idle for an hour.

3. Your mobile data usage suddenly jumps

Spyware and malware send your photos, messages and location to a remote server, and that uses mobile data. If your data package that used to last the whole month now runs out in a week, check Settings → Data usage (Android) or Settings → Mobile Data (iPhone) and look for an unfamiliar app consuming large amounts of data.

4. Apps appear that you never installed

This is one of the clearest signs. Open your app drawer and look for apps you do not recognise — especially ones with generic names, no icon, or names that look like system apps but are spelled slightly wrong. Malware often installs extra apps or hides itself under a boring name like "System Update" or "Settings Service".

5. Pop-ups appear even when no app is open

Advertisements popping up on your home screen, over other apps, or even on the lock screen usually mean adware has been installed. Real apps show ads inside themselves; they do not push pop-ups across your whole phone. Do not tap these pop-ups — many of them try to install more malware.

6. The phone is slow, freezes or restarts by itself

Malware eats memory and processor power, so the phone lags, apps crash, and the phone sometimes restarts on its own. A single crash means nothing, but daily freezing on a phone that used to run smoothly is worth checking — especially combined with any other sign on this list.

7. Strange calls, texts or messages go out from your number

Friends tell you they received a weird link from you, or your call history shows calls you never made, or your sent folder contains texts you never wrote. Some malware sends premium-rate texts that quietly charge your balance, or spreads phishing links to all your contacts. Check your call log and sent messages today.

8. Settings change by themselves

Your screen lock is off when you always keep it on. A new Wi-Fi network is saved. Battery saver or mobile data is switched off. An unknown device appears in your Google or Apple account. Your browser homepage or search engine has changed. You did not do any of this — so something or someone else did.

9. You are locked out of your own accounts

Your Google, Apple, Facebook or WhatsApp password suddenly does not work, or you receive password-reset emails you never requested. This often means an attacker has already taken over an account using information stolen from your phone. Act fast — our hacked social media account recovery guide walks through getting an account back step by step.

10. The camera or microphone turns on by itself

On Android and iPhone, a small green or orange dot appears at the top of the screen when the camera or microphone is in use. If that dot appears while you are not on a call, not recording and not using any camera app, something is using it without your knowledge. Check which app was using it in your privacy settings.

11. Strange charges or missing money

Small charges on your mobile balance, subscriptions you never bought, or money moving out of a bank account or mobile wallet are serious signs. Some malware signs you up for premium services in the background. Check your balance, your bank statement and your app store subscriptions today — and if money has moved, call your bank immediately and freeze the account.

12. Your contacts receive scam messages "from you"

If several contacts report the same strange message from your number or your WhatsApp, your account or your phone is being used to spread scams. Warn your contacts, change your passwords from another device, and check the linked-devices list in WhatsApp and other messaging apps for sessions you do not recognise.

First, rule out the innocent explanations

Before you panic, check the boring causes — most strange phone behaviour has a harmless reason:

  • An old battery. A phone battery that is two or three years old holds less charge and drains fast. Check battery health in settings before assuming malware.
  • A recent update. A new Android or iOS update can make a phone hot and slow for a day while it re-indexes files. If the problems started the day after an update and fade within 48 hours, that is usually the cause.
  • One badly made app. A single buggy game or free app can drain battery, show ads and slow the phone. Uninstall the most recent apps one by one and see if the problem stops.
  • Weak signal. A phone constantly searching for signal in a low-coverage area drains battery and heats up fast.

If you find an innocent cause, fix it and keep watching for a week. If you cannot find one — or if two or more signs from the list above are present — treat the phone as compromised and work through the steps below.

What to do right now: the step-by-step cleanup

Work through these steps in order. Do not skip ahead to the factory reset — the earlier steps protect your accounts first, so the attacker cannot lock you out while you clean the phone.

  1. Turn on airplane mode — but stay calm. Airplane mode cuts the phone off the internet, which stops malware sending your data out and stops an attacker giving it new orders. You can still use the phone's settings while offline. Do not turn the phone off completely yet.
  2. Do not log in to your bank or email on this phone. Anything you type could be recorded. Use a different device — a family member's phone or a computer — for all password changes until the phone is clean.
  3. Check for device administrator apps (Android). Go to Settings → Security → Device admin apps and look for anything unfamiliar. Malware often gives itself administrator rights so you cannot uninstall it. Switch off admin rights for any app you do not recognise, then uninstall it.
  4. Uninstall suspicious apps. Delete every app you do not recognise and every app installed around the time the problems started. On iPhone, long-press the app and tap Remove App; on Android, use Settings → Apps so you can see apps that hide their icon.
  5. Run a scan. On Android, open the Play Store → your profile → Play Protect and tap Scan. It checks installed apps against Google's list of known harmful apps. Our malware removal guide explains deeper cleaning steps if Play Protect finds something — or finds nothing but the signs remain.
  6. Change your passwords — from another device. Start with your email account, because email is the key to resetting every other account. Then change your Google or Apple account, your bank, and your social media passwords. Use a different, strong password for each one. Our password manager guide shows how to do this without having to memorise them all.
  7. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere. Two-factor authentication means a stolen password alone is not enough to open your account. Our two-factor authentication guide walks through setting it up on Google, Apple, Facebook and WhatsApp.
  8. Check your accounts' active sessions. In your Google account, check Security → Your devices; in Apple, check Settings → [your name]; in WhatsApp, check Settings → Linked devices. Sign out every device or session you do not recognise.
  9. Check your money. Look at your bank statement, mobile wallet and app store subscriptions for the last month. Dispute anything unfamiliar with your bank, and cancel any subscription you did not buy.
  10. Update the phone, then decide about a factory reset. Install every pending system update. If the signs are gone and nothing suspicious remains, keep watching the phone for a week. If the signs remain — or if banking details were involved — do a factory reset: back up only your photos and contacts first (not apps), then reset the phone and set it up as a new device instead of restoring a full backup, so the malware cannot come back with it. Our guide to wiping a phone safely explains the reset process step by step.

Android vs iPhone: what is different?

Both can be compromised, but the risks and the checks are slightly different:

  • Android is more often targeted, because apps can be installed from outside the Play Store. The biggest protection is simple: install apps only from the Play Store, keep Play Protect on, and never enable "Install unknown apps" for a browser or messaging app.
  • iPhone is harder to infect because apps are locked to the App Store and the system is closed. Most iPhone compromises are not malware on the phone itself — they are stolen Apple ID passwords, phishing, or a fake profile installed from a website. Check Settings → General → VPN & Device Management for any profile you did not install, and check your Apple ID devices list.
  • On both phones, the account is usually the weak point, not the device. A stolen password with no two-factor authentication is the most common way an attacker gets into someone's photos, chats and money — and it works on any phone.

How to stop it happening again

Cleaning the phone is only half the job. These five habits stop most attacks before they start:

  • Update the phone the day an update arrives. Updates fix security holes. Turn on automatic updates so you never forget.
  • Install apps only from the official store. Never install an APK sent on WhatsApp, a "free" version of a paid app, or an app from a random website. If an app asks for permissions it does not need — a torch app asking for your contacts — delete it.
  • Use a screen lock and keep the phone with you. A PIN, fingerprint or face lock stops someone installing spyware in the minute your phone is out of your sight.
  • Never tap links in unexpected messages. Banks, courier companies and prize offers do not send login links by SMS or WhatsApp. If a message worries you, open the official app yourself — never use the link in the message.
  • Back up before trouble strikes. A clean backup means a factory reset costs you nothing. Our data backup guide shows the simple 3-2-1 rule for protecting your photos, contacts and files.

When to report it and get help

Some situations are bigger than a cleanup, and you should not handle them alone:

  • Money was stolen. Call your bank first and freeze the account, then report it. In Pakistan, cybercrime can be reported to the FIA Cybercrime Wing — our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and what evidence to keep.
  • Someone is threatening you or sharing your private photos. Save screenshots of everything, do not delete the evidence, and report it to the FIA and to the platform where it is happening.
  • The phone was a work phone or holds other people's data. Tell your employer or organisation immediately — they have procedures for this, and waiting makes it worse.
  • You suspect spyware from someone you know. Stalkerware — software installed by a partner, family member or employer to watch you — is a real problem. A factory reset removes it, but change all your passwords from a different device first, and consider speaking to a trusted person or a support organisation about the situation.
  • The signs remain after a factory reset. This is rare, but it can mean the account itself is compromised rather than the phone. Change every password from a clean device, turn on two-factor authentication, and ask a professional repair shop to check the phone.

Key Takeaways

  • One sign means little; two or three together mean act today. Battery drain, heat, data spikes, mystery apps and strange messages are the most common clues.
  • Rule out innocent causes first — an old battery, a recent update or one buggy app — but do not explain away several signs at once.
  • Cut the connection, then protect your accounts first — airplane mode, then change passwords and turn on two-factor authentication from another device.
  • Remove suspicious apps and run a scan before deciding whether a factory reset is needed.
  • If money moved, call your bank immediately and report the crime to the FIA Cybercrime Wing.
  • Prevention is simple: update on time, install apps only from the official store, use a screen lock, and never tap links in unexpected messages.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your own phone and accounts, with no attempt to access anyone else's device.
