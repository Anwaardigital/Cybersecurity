How to Secure Your Facebook Account: Stop Hackers, Scams and Cloned Profiles (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 21 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 11 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Facebook is still the most-used social network in Pakistan — for family, friends, business pages, buy-and-sell groups, and Marketplace. That popularity is exactly why criminals target it. A stolen Facebook account is valuable to a thief: with your account they can message your family pretending to be you and ask for emergency money, post scams in your groups, take over your business page, steal your Marketplace customers, and read years of your private messages and photos. And Facebook scams are getting more convincing every year: cloned profiles that copy your photo and name, fake "your account will be deleted in 24 hours" warnings, fake copyright-strike messages, and Marketplace buyers who "accidentally" overpay you. The good news is that Facebook has strong built-in protections — two-factor authentication, login alerts, session controls, and fine-grained privacy settings — but most people never turn them on. This guide explains how Facebook accounts actually get stolen, the warning signs, and the exact settings to lock down today, plus what to do if your account is already hacked. Everything here is defensive: protecting your own account, your own family, and your own business.

How Facebook accounts get stolen

Criminals rarely break into Facebook's servers. Instead, they trick you, or trick the systems around your account. These are the five methods used most often:

  • The fake login page (phishing — the most common). You get a message, email, or notification: "Your account will be deleted", "Copyright violation — verify now", or "You won a prize". The link opens a page that looks exactly like Facebook's login screen. You type your email and password, and the thief captures them. The real Facebook login page is only at facebook.com or in the official Facebook app — never a link someone sent you. Our fake copyright-strike guide explains one common version of this trick in detail.
  • Cloned profiles and impersonation. The thief copies your profile photo, your name, and your cover photo, and creates a new account that looks like you. Then they send friend requests to your friends and family, and message them asking for money, a mobile top-up, or a "help me, I am in trouble" favour. Your real friends cannot tell the fake account from the real one at a glance. This is one of the most reported Facebook scams in Pakistan.
  • Password reuse. If you use the same password on Facebook that you used on another website, and that website leaks its passwords in a data breach, the thief simply tries the same password on Facebook. It works far more often than people think. Our password security guide explains why every account needs its own password, and our password manager guide shows how to manage them safely.
  • Fake apps, quizzes and "who viewed your profile" traps. "See who visited your profile", "Find out your future husband", "Free Facebook followers" — these apps and quizzes ask you to log in with Facebook or give them broad permissions. Many of them harvest your friend list, your photos, and your access token, which lets the thief act as you. Facebook does not show who viewed your profile, and no quiz can tell you your future.
  • SIM swapping and stolen login codes. If your Facebook login code goes to your phone number by SMS, a criminal who takes over your SIM can receive your codes. Our SIM swapping protection guide explains how to stop this.

7 warning signs your Facebook account is compromised

If you notice any of these, check your account immediately using the steps below:

  • Your password suddenly stops working and Facebook says it is incorrect, even though you typed it correctly. This is the clearest sign someone changed it.
  • Your email address or phone number on the account changed without you changing it, or you get an email saying your password or email was changed that you did not request.
  • Posts, messages, or friend requests appear that you never made — especially messages asking your contacts for money or sharing strange links.
  • Your friends tell you they got a new friend request from "you", or a second profile with your photo and name appears. That is a cloned profile — report it (Step 6).
  • You see an unknown device or location in your "Where you're logged in" list — a phone, computer, or city you do not recognise.
  • Your profile photo, name, or bio changed without you changing it, or new pages, groups, or ads appeared that you never created.
  • Login alert emails or codes arrive when you did not try to log in. Someone just tried to log in as you. Do not ignore these — turn on two-factor authentication right now.

If several of these apply, treat your account as stolen and jump to the recovery section at the end of this guide. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the wider warning signs that apply to your whole phone as well.

Step 1: Turn on two-factor authentication (the single most important step)

Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second check after your password — a code from an authenticator app or a text message. Even if a thief steals your password, they cannot log in without that code. Turn it on now; it takes less than two minutes:

  1. Open Facebook, tap your profile picture (top right on the app, or the menu), then Settings & privacy, then Settings.
  2. Go to Accounts Centre, then Password and security.
  3. Tap Two-factor authentication and choose your account.
  4. Pick Authentication app (the safest choice — Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, or any similar app) or Text message (SMS). An authenticator app is safer than SMS because it cannot be stolen by SIM swapping.
  5. Follow the steps to link the app or confirm your phone number, then save the recovery codes Facebook shows you. Keep these codes somewhere safe — on paper, or in a password manager — because they are your way back in if you lose your phone.

After this, every new login needs your password and a code from your phone. A thief with only your password is stopped. Our two-factor authentication guide explains the same idea for your Google, WhatsApp and banking accounts.

Step 2: Use a strong, unique password — and change it if it ever leaked

Your Facebook password should exist nowhere else. If you used the same password on another site that leaked, change your Facebook password today:

  • Make it long, not clever. A password of 16 or more characters — three or four random words joined together, for example — is far stronger than a short password with symbols. Avoid your name, your birthday, your children's names, or 123456.
  • Never reuse it. One password per account. A password manager remembers them all so you only remember one. Our password manager guide shows how to set one up.
  • Change it after any breach. If you hear that a website you use was hacked, change your Facebook password even if Facebook was not the site that leaked. Our data breach recovery guide explains what to do after a leak.
  • Do not share your password with anyone — not a friend, not a "Facebook agent", not a shop that offers to "boost" your page. Facebook staff will never ask for your password.

Step 3: Check where you are logged in — and log out strangers

Facebook lets you see every phone, tablet and computer logged into your account. A thief who logs in on their own device can read your messages without you knowing. Checking takes one minute:

  1. Open Facebook, go to Settings & privacy > Settings > Accounts Centre > Password and security.
  2. Tap Where you're logged in. You will see every device, with its location and last active time.
  3. Look at every entry. You should recognise each one — your own phone, your own laptop. If you see a device, browser, or city you do not recognise, tap it and choose Log out immediately.
  4. If you find a session you do not recognise, also change your password and turn on two-factor authentication (Steps 1 and 2), because someone had access to your account.
  5. Turn on login alerts in the same menu, so Facebook tells you by email or notification every time a new device logs in.

Make this a monthly habit — the same way you check your bank balance. A device you did not log in from is proof your account was accessed, even if nothing else looks wrong.

Step 4: Lock down your privacy settings

Your privacy settings decide what strangers can see and do to you on Facebook. Go to Settings & privacy > Privacy Checkup and set these:

  • Who can see your posts: "Friends" (not "Public"). Public posts are visible to anyone on the internet — including scammers building a cloned profile from your photos and details. Set your default audience to Friends, and check old public posts too: in Privacy Checkup you can limit past posts to Friends in one tap.
  • Who can send you friend requests: "Friends of friends". This cuts most fake friend requests from strangers, which are the first step of most Facebook scams.
  • Who can look you up by email or phone number: "Friends" or "Only me". Your phone number is the key to your account. Strangers do not need to find you with it.
  • Profile and tagging: turn on "Review posts you're tagged in". This stops a scammer tagging you in a scam post that then appears to your friends as if you shared it.
  • Face recognition: off. Facebook's face recognition can identify you in photos other people upload. Turning it off keeps your face out of that system.
  • Location: off for Facebook. In your phone settings, turn off Facebook's location access, or set it to "While using the app" at most. Your location history tells a thief where you live, work, and travel.
  • Block and report, don't argue. If a stranger messages you with a prize, a job offer, or a threat, do not reply. Open their profile, tap the three dots, choose Block, then Report profile. Reporting helps Facebook ban the account.

Step 5: Spot the scams that target Facebook users

Most Facebook scams follow a small number of scripts. Once you know them, they are easy to spot:

  • Cloned profiles asking for money. A copy of your friend's (or your own) profile messages you: "I am in trouble, send me money urgently" or "Send me a JazzCash/Easypaisa transfer". Before sending any money, call the person on their real number. A voice or video call takes thirty seconds and stops this scam completely.
  • "Your account will be deleted" warnings. A message or notification claims your page violated a policy and will be deleted in 24 hours unless you click a link and "verify". Facebook does not delete accounts this way, and it does not send warnings through random pages or Messenger. Delete the message.
  • Fake copyright strikes. "Your video violated copyright, appeal here" — with a link to a fake login page. Our fake copyright-strike guide explains this trick in full.
  • Marketplace scams. A "buyer" offers to pay extra and asks you to click a link to "confirm" the payment, or asks for a small "delivery fee" first. A "seller" offers a phone or bike far below market price and asks for advance payment. On Marketplace, meet in person, in a public place, and never pay in advance. Our safe online shopping guide covers this in detail.
  • Fake giveaways and "free" offers. "Win a free iPhone", "Free Umrah package", "Government grant — apply now". Real giveaways never ask you to pay a fee, share a code, or log in through a link. Any message asking you to send money to receive money is a scam.
  • Fake job offers and "online earning" groups. A message offers easy work from home, then asks for a "registration fee" or your bank details. Real employers do not hire over Messenger and never ask for a fee. Our fake job offers guide explains the job-scam version of this trick in detail.
  • Romance and "friendship" scams. A stranger builds a friendly relationship over weeks, then asks for money for a "medical emergency" or a "ticket to visit you". Our romance scams guide explains how this trap works.

The rule for all of these is the same: slow down. Scammers create urgency — "now", "today only", "limited offer" — because a person in a hurry does not think. If a message makes you feel rushed or frightened, stop, and check it through a different channel: call the person, visit the official website yourself, or ask a family member.

Step 6: Protect your friends from your cloned profile

You cannot stop a thief copying your public photo — but you can make it much harder, and you can make sure your friends spot the fake:

  • Turn on Profile Picture Guard. On the Facebook app, open your profile picture, tap the three dots, and turn on Profile Picture Guard. This stops other people downloading or screenshotting your profile photo easily, which is how clones are usually built.
  • Keep your friend list private. In your privacy settings, set "Who can see your friends list" to "Only me". A thief who can see your friend list can send them all friend requests from the fake profile.
  • Tell your friends how to spot the clone. A real friend request from you will come from your old account, not a brand-new one with few friends and few posts. Ask your family to check: if the account was created recently, has almost no friends, and only your photo — it is fake.
  • Report a clone immediately. If you find a fake copy of your profile, open it, tap the three dots, choose Find support or report profile, then Pretending to be someone, then Me. Ask your friends to report it too — several reports get it removed faster. Do the same for a cloned profile of a friend, choosing A friend instead.

Step 7: Keep your phone and the Facebook app safe

Your Facebook account is only as safe as the phone it lives on:

  • Only install the official Facebook app from the Google Play Store or Apple App Store. Never install a "Facebook Lite mod", "Facebook++", or any app that promises extra Facebook features — many of them steal your login session. Our fake apps guide explains how lookalike apps steal your account.
  • Keep Facebook updated. Updates fix security holes. Turn on automatic updates for Facebook in your app store, or check for updates every week.
  • Lock your phone. A thief with your unlocked phone can open Facebook and change your password, your email, and your recovery options. Use a PIN, fingerprint, or face lock, and turn on Facebook's own app lock if your phone supports it.
  • Log out on shared devices. If you ever log into Facebook on a friend's phone, a cybercafe computer, or a shop's computer, log out before you leave — and never tick "Remember me" or "Keep me logged in" on a device that is not yours. Our public computer safety guide explains this in detail.
  • Check app permissions. In your phone settings, look at what Facebook can access. It needs storage and camera to work. It does not need your SMS messages or your call logs. If an app calling itself Facebook asks for those, it is fake — uninstall it immediately.

Your account was hacked — recover it now

If you are locked out of Facebook, act quickly — the thief is messaging your contacts right now. Do these steps in order:

  1. Go to facebook.com/hacked. This is Facebook's official recovery page. Enter your email address or phone number, and follow the steps. Facebook will ask you to confirm your identity — often with a code sent to your email or phone.
  2. Change your password immediately once you are back in. Pick a new, strong, unique password (Step 2).
  3. Log out all other sessions. Go to Settings > Accounts Centre > Password and security > Where you're logged in and tap Log out of all sessions. This logs the thief out of your account on every device except yours.
  4. Turn on two-factor authentication if you did not have it on (Step 1). Also check that your email address and phone number on the account are still yours — if the thief changed them, add your real ones back.
  5. Warn your contacts immediately. As soon as you are back in, post on your timeline and message your family: "My Facebook was hacked. Do not send money or click links from my account before today." The thief's most common trick is asking your contacts for emergency money in your name.
  6. Check your pages and groups. If you run a page or group, check that the thief did not add themselves as an admin, post scam links, or change the page details. Remove any admin you do not recognise and delete scam posts.
  7. Report the crime. In Pakistan, Facebook hacking and the fraud that follows it can be reported to the FIA Cybercrime Wing. Keep screenshots of the scam messages, the thief's profile link, and any money-transfer details. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.

Key Takeaways

  • Turn on two-factor authentication today — a password plus a code from your phone stops most Facebook theft. Use an authenticator app if you can.
  • Use a strong, unique password that you use nowhere else, and change it after any data breach.
  • Check "Where you're logged in" every month and log out any device you do not recognise.
  • Set your posts to "Friends", your friend list to "Only me", and turn on Profile Picture Guard.
  • Never click a "your account will be deleted" link — Facebook does not warn you that way, and the link leads to a fake login page.
  • Before sending money to anyone on Facebook, call them on their real number first — a thirty-second call stops the cloned-profile scam.
  • If your account is hacked, go to facebook.com/hacked, change your password, log out all sessions, and warn your contacts — then report it to Facebook and the FIA.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your own account, your own family, and your own business from scammers.
