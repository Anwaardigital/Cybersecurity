How to Secure Your WhatsApp Account: Stop Hackers, Scams and Hijacking (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 16 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 10 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

For most people in Pakistan, WhatsApp is not just an app — it is where family groups live, where business orders arrive, where school announcements come, and where photos of children are shared every day. That is exactly why criminals want your WhatsApp account. A stolen WhatsApp account is worth money to a scammer: they can message your contacts pretending to be you, ask your family for emergency money, spread fake links to your whole contact list, and read your private chats. And the worst part is how easy it is to lose: in most cases the victim hands the thief the key themselves, by sharing a six-digit code they received by SMS. This guide explains how WhatsApp accounts actually get stolen, the warning signs that yours is in danger, and the exact settings to turn on today — two-step verification, linked-device checks, and privacy controls — plus what to do if your account is already stolen. Everything here is defensive: protecting your own account, your own chats, and your own contacts.

How WhatsApp accounts get stolen

Criminals rarely "hack" WhatsApp itself. Instead, they trick you, or trick the systems around your account. These are the four methods used most often in Pakistan:

  • The verification code trick (the most common). The thief enters your phone number on their own phone. WhatsApp sends you a six-digit registration code by SMS. Then the thief messages you — often pretending to be a friend, a delivery rider, or WhatsApp support — and asks you to forward that code "to confirm your delivery" or "to fix an error". The moment you share the code, they register your number on their phone and you are locked out. WhatsApp will never ask you for your code, and no genuine friend needs it.
  • SIM swapping. The criminal convinces your mobile company to issue a replacement SIM for your number, or steals your phone and uses the SIM in another phone. With your number in their hands, they receive your WhatsApp registration code themselves. Our SIM swapping protection guide explains how to stop this.
  • The linked-device QR scam. The thief sends you a QR code — in a message, a poster, or a "free gift" link — and asks you to scan it with WhatsApp's "Link a device" feature. Scanning it links YOUR WhatsApp to THEIR computer, and they can read your chats in real time. Never scan a WhatsApp QR code sent to you by a stranger, and never scan one to "win" anything.
  • Fake support and prize messages. "Your WhatsApp account will be deleted in 24 hours, click here to verify." "You won a prize, confirm your account." These messages push you to a fake login page or ask for your code. WhatsApp does not delete accounts this way, and it does not give prizes by message. Our guide to phone scams covers the same tricks on calls.

7 warning signs your WhatsApp account is compromised

If you notice any of these, check your account immediately using the steps below:

  • You are logged out of WhatsApp suddenly and it asks you to register again, even though you did nothing. This is the clearest sign someone else registered your number.
  • Messages appear as "read" that you never opened, or replies were sent from your account that you did not write.
  • Your contacts receive strange messages from you — asking for money, sharing links, or offering prizes. If a friend says "you sent me a weird link", believe them.
  • Your profile photo, name, or "About" text changed without you changing it.
  • You see an unknown device in your linked devices list — a computer or browser session you do not recognise.
  • Your WhatsApp registration code arrives by SMS when you did not request it. Someone just tried to register your number. Do not share that code with anyone, and turn on two-step verification right now.
  • You are added to groups you never joined, or your privacy settings changed by themselves.

If several of these apply, treat your account as stolen and jump to the recovery section at the end of this guide. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the wider warning signs that apply to your whole phone as well.

Step 1: Turn on two-step verification (the single most important step)

Two-step verification adds a six-digit PIN that anyone registering your number must enter — even if they have your SMS code. Without it, a stolen SMS code is enough to take your account. With it, the thief is stopped. Turn it on now; it takes less than a minute:

  1. Open WhatsApp, tap the three dots (Android) or Settings (iPhone), then Settings.
  2. Tap Account, then Two-step verification, then Turn on.
  3. Choose a six-digit PIN you will remember. Do not use your birthday, your phone number, or 123456 — pick something only you know. Write it down somewhere safe at home, not in your phone's notes app.
  4. Add your email address when WhatsApp asks. This email lets you reset the PIN if you forget it. Use an email account you actually control, and make sure that email itself has a strong password and two-factor authentication.
  5. Confirm the PIN and the email. Done — your account is now far harder to steal.

WhatsApp will ask you for this PIN from time to time, so you do not forget it. If you ever forget the PIN and you did not add an email, you will be locked out of your account for seven days — so the email step matters. Our two-factor authentication guide explains the same idea for your Google, Facebook and banking accounts, and our password manager guide shows how to store PINs safely.

Step 2: Never share your verification code — with anyone

This is the one rule that stops most WhatsApp theft. The six-digit code WhatsApp sends by SMS is the key to your account. Treat it like your bank card PIN:

  • Never share the code, even with someone you know. If a friend's account is stolen, the thief messages all of their contacts asking for codes — so the request may come from a real friend's number. If anyone asks for your WhatsApp code for any reason, it is a scam. A real friend never needs your code.
  • Never say the code out loud on a call. Scammers call pretending to be from a delivery company, a bank, or WhatsApp itself, and ask you to read the code that just arrived. WhatsApp staff will never call you and ask for a code.
  • Do not type the code into any website or form. A link that asks for your WhatsApp verification code is a trap. Close it.
  • If a code arrives and you did not ask for one, someone is trying to steal your account right now. Do not panic and do not share it. Open WhatsApp and check your linked devices (Step 3), and make sure two-step verification is on (Step 1).

Step 3: Check your linked devices every week

WhatsApp lets you use your account on a computer or another phone through "Linked devices". A thief who links their computer can read your messages without you knowing — and you will not be logged out. Checking takes thirty seconds:

  1. Open WhatsApp, tap the three dots (Android) or Settings (iPhone).
  2. Tap Linked devices.
  3. Look at every device in the list. You should recognise each one — your own laptop, your own tablet. If you see a device, browser, or location you do not recognise, tap it and choose Log out immediately.
  4. If you find a device you do not recognise, also change your two-step verification PIN and check your privacy settings, because someone had access to your account.

Make this a weekly habit — the same way you check your bank balance. A linked device you did not add is proof your account was accessed, even if nothing else looks wrong.

Step 4: Lock down your privacy settings

Your privacy settings decide what strangers can see and do to you on WhatsApp. Go to Settings > Privacy and set these:

  • Last seen and online: "My contacts" or "Nobody". Strangers do not need to know when you are online. Scammers watch "last seen" to find active targets.
  • Profile photo: "My contacts". Your photo is used to impersonate you — a scammer copies your photo and your name, then messages your family asking for money. Keeping your photo visible only to contacts makes this harder.
  • About: "My contacts". The same reason — your About text and photo together make a convincing fake account.
  • Groups: "My contacts". This stops strangers adding you to scam groups — fake investment groups, prize groups, and loan groups are a major problem in Pakistan. With this setting, a stranger must send you an invite instead of adding you directly.
  • Status: "My contacts" (or "My contacts except..."). Only people you know should see your daily updates.
  • Silence unknown callers: turn it on. In Settings > Privacy > Calls, turn on "Silence unknown callers". Scam and spam calls from unknown numbers will not ring — you can still see them in your call list and call back if it was genuine.
  • Block and report, don't argue. If a stranger messages you with a prize, a job offer, or a threat, do not reply. Tap their name, choose Block, and tick Report. Reporting helps WhatsApp ban the number. Our fake job offers guide explains the job-scam version of this trick in detail.

Step 5: Spot the scams that target WhatsApp users

Most WhatsApp scams follow a small number of scripts. Once you know them, they are easy to spot:

  • "Your WhatsApp will be deleted" or "verify your account now". WhatsApp does not threaten to delete your account by message, and it never asks you to click a link to verify. Delete the message and block the number.
  • "I am your son/daughter/brother, I lost my phone, send money." A scammer messages parents from a new number claiming to be their child in an emergency. Before sending any money, call your child on their real number — or call another family member to check. A voice or video call takes thirty seconds and stops this scam completely.
  • Prize, lottery and cashback messages. "You won Rs. 50,000" or "claim your free gift" — with a link. There is no prize. The link installs malware or steals your details. Delete it.
  • Fake job offers and "online earning" groups. A message offers easy work from home, then asks for a "registration fee" or your bank details. Real employers do not hire over WhatsApp and never ask for a fee.
  • Investment and trading groups. You are added to a group where everyone claims to be making money from crypto or forex. The "winners" are fake accounts run by the scammers. The moment you deposit money, it is gone.
  • Links that look like WhatsApp itself. A message contains a link to "whatsapp-support" or "whatsapp-verify" on a strange web address. Real WhatsApp pages live on whatsapp.com. Anything else is fake — check the address carefully before you tap.

The rule for all of these is the same: slow down. Scammers create urgency — "now", "today only", "your account will close" — because a person in a hurry does not think. If a message makes you feel rushed or frightened, stop, and check it through a different channel: call the person, visit the official website yourself, or ask a family member.

Step 6: Back up safely and lock your chats

Your chats contain years of family photos and important messages. Protect them:

  • Turn on end-to-end encrypted backup. In Settings > Chats > Chat backup > End-to-end encrypted backup, turn it on and set a password or 64-digit key. Without this, your backup stored on Google Drive or iCloud can be read by anyone who gets into that cloud account. With it, only you can restore it. Our back-up guide explains the wider 3-2-1 backup rule for your phone and computer.
  • Use Chat Lock for sensitive chats. Long-press a chat, choose Lock chat, and it moves into a locked folder that needs your fingerprint or PIN to open. Useful if someone else sometimes holds your phone.
  • Use disappearing messages for sensitive conversations. In a chat, tap the contact's name, choose Disappearing messages, and pick 24 hours, 7 days, or 90 days. Messages delete themselves automatically — less history for anyone who grabs your phone to read.
  • Lock WhatsApp itself. On Android, use your phone's app lock, or set WhatsApp's own fingerprint lock in Settings > Privacy > Fingerprint lock. A locked phone plus a locked WhatsApp means a thief with your phone still cannot read your chats.

Your account was stolen — recover it now

If you are locked out of your WhatsApp, act quickly — the thief is messaging your contacts right now. Do these steps in order:

  1. Re-register your number. Uninstall WhatsApp, install it again, and register with your phone number. You will receive a six-digit code by SMS. Enter it. This logs the thief out of your account on their device. If two-step verification is on and you know the PIN, enter it when asked. If you do not know the PIN — because the thief set one — you will have to wait seven days before you can register without it, unless you use the email reset option.
  2. If you cannot get the SMS code, wait and retry. If the thief requested codes many times, WhatsApp may temporarily block new codes. Wait for the timer to finish, then request a voice call code instead — WhatsApp can call you and read the code aloud.
  3. Warn your contacts immediately. As soon as you are back in, post a status and message your family groups: "My WhatsApp was stolen. Do not send money or click links from my number before today." The thief's most common trick is asking your contacts for emergency money in your name.
  4. Check linked devices and change your PIN. Go to Linked devices and log out of every device. Then change your two-step verification PIN and add a recovery email if you have not already.
  5. Email WhatsApp support. If you still cannot recover the account, email support@whatsapp.com from your email address, with your phone number in full international format (for example, +92 3XX XXXXXXX) and a short description of what happened. Ask them to deactivate the account if you cannot recover it — a deactivated account cannot message your contacts.
  6. Report the crime. In Pakistan, WhatsApp hijacking and the fraud that follows it can be reported to the FIA Cybercrime Wing. Keep screenshots of the scam messages, the thief's number, and any money-transfer details. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.

Key Takeaways

  • Turn on two-step verification today — a six-digit PIN plus a recovery email stops most WhatsApp theft. It takes less than a minute.
  • Never share your six-digit verification code with anyone, for any reason. WhatsApp will never ask for it, and no real friend needs it.
  • Check your linked devices every week and log out of anything you do not recognise.
  • Set your privacy to "My contacts" for your photo, last seen, About and groups, and turn on "Silence unknown callers".
  • Slow down when a message creates urgency — verify through a voice or video call before you send money or share a code.
  • If your account is stolen, re-register immediately, warn your contacts, log out all linked devices, and report it to WhatsApp and the FIA.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your own account, your own chats, and your own family from scammers.
