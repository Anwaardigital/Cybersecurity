Passkeys Explained: How to Set Up Passwordless Login Safely (2026)

Last updated: 9 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 11 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Passwords have been the key to our accounts for decades, and they have also been the weakest part of our security for just as long. People reuse them, choose weak ones, write them on paper, and type them into fake login pages built by scammers. Passkeys are the industry's answer: a way to log in that has no password to steal, guess, or phish. Google, Apple and Microsoft all support them now, and most major websites are adding them. This guide explains, in plain language, what a passkey is, how it protects you, and how to set one up safely on the accounts that matter most — your email, your bank, and your social accounts. Everything here is defensive: it is about protecting your own accounts, never about getting into anyone else's.

What a passkey actually is

A passkey is a pair of cryptographic keys that replaces your password. When you create a passkey for a website, your phone or computer generates two mathematically linked keys: a private key that never leaves your device, and a public key that is sent to the website. To log in, the website sends your device a challenge — a random puzzle — and your device signs it with the private key. The website checks the signature against the public key, and if it matches, you are in.

Three things make this different from a password. First, there is nothing to remember and nothing to type, so there is nothing for a scammer to trick out of you. Second, the private key never travels over the internet, so it cannot be intercepted or stolen from a server breach. Third, the passkey is tied to the real website: your device will only use it for the genuine site, so a fake lookalike login page cannot use it at all. That last point is the most important one — passkeys are resistant to phishing by design, which is something no password, however strong, can claim.

You still unlock the passkey with something you have — your fingerprint, your face, or your device PIN — so the everyday experience feels familiar: tap your finger, and you are logged in. The difference is what happens behind the scenes. Instead of sending a shared secret to the website, your device proves it holds the private key without ever revealing it.

Passkeys vs passwords vs two-factor authentication

It helps to see where passkeys sit next to the protections you already know. A password is a secret you share with the website — and anything shared can be stolen, guessed, or phished. Two-factor authentication adds a second check, usually a code from an app or a message, which protects you even if the password leaks. A passkey combines the jobs of both: it is stronger than a password alone, and it already includes the proof that you hold the device, which is what the second factor was doing.

That does not mean you should switch everything off. For accounts that support passkeys, use the passkey as your main login and keep two-factor authentication switched on as well where it is offered — the two protections stack, and the cost to you is one extra tap. For accounts that do not offer passkeys yet, a strong unique password stored in a password manager, plus two-factor authentication, remains the right answer. Our guides on password security and two-factor authentication explain that setup in detail.

Setting up a passkey on Google

Your Google account is the most important account to protect with a passkey, because it guards your email, your photos, and the recovery path for many other accounts. The setup takes about two minutes:

  1. On your phone or computer, open your Google account settings and go to Security.
  2. Find Passkeys (sometimes listed under "How you sign in to Google") and tap Create a passkey.
  3. Confirm it is you with your fingerprint, face, or device PIN.
  4. Your device creates the passkey and stores it in your password manager — Google Password Manager on Android and Chrome, or iCloud Keychain on Apple devices.
  5. Sign out and sign back in once to test it: you should be offered the passkey instead of your password.

Do not delete your password immediately after creating the passkey. Keep the password as a backup sign-in method for a few weeks, until you are sure the passkey works on every device you use. A passkey stored only on one phone is a single point of failure — the next section explains how to avoid that.

Setting up passkeys on Apple and Microsoft

On Apple devices, passkeys are built into the system. When a website or app offers to create a passkey, your iPhone or Mac will ask whether to save it in iCloud Keychain. Accept, confirm with Face ID or Touch ID, and the passkey is stored and synced to your other Apple devices signed in to the same Apple ID. Your Apple ID itself can also use a passkey for sign-in, which you can enable in your Apple ID security settings.

On Microsoft accounts, sign in at your Microsoft account security page, open Ways to sign in, and choose to add a passkey or a security key. Microsoft will guide you through creating the passkey on your phone or computer. Windows Hello — the fingerprint, face, or PIN you use to unlock your PC — can serve as the way you approve passkeys on that computer.

The pattern is the same everywhere: the site offers a passkey, your device creates and stores it, and you approve it with your fingerprint, face, or PIN. If a site you use offers a passkey option in its security settings, take it — the setup is always short, and the protection is a genuine step up from a password.

Synced passkeys vs device-bound passkeys

Most passkeys you create are synced: they are stored in a password manager — Google Password Manager, iCloud Keychain, or a third-party password manager — and copied to your other devices signed in to the same account. This is convenient, and it is also your safety net: if you lose your phone, your passkeys are still on your other devices.

Some passkeys are device-bound: they live only on one device, often a hardware security key such as a USB key, and cannot be copied. These are stronger in one sense — there is no synced copy to attack — but they are also riskier for everyday use, because losing the device means losing access. For most people, synced passkeys in a reputable password manager are the right balance. The rule to remember is simple: a passkey is only as recoverable as the account that syncs it, so protect that account — your Google or Apple account — with its own strong sign-in and recovery options.

What to do if you lose your phone

Losing your phone is the scenario people worry about most with passkeys, and it is worth planning for before it happens:

  • Keep more than one device signed in. If your passkeys sync through Google or Apple, they are already on your other signed-in devices. Sign in on a second device now, and confirm the passkeys appear there.
  • Keep a backup sign-in method. Leave your password in place as a fallback on important accounts, and keep a second two-factor method — an authenticator app on another device, or backup codes printed and stored safely at home.
  • Keep your recovery information current. A recovery email address and a recovery phone number you still control are what let you prove you are you. Check them once a year — an old number you no longer own is the most common reason account recovery fails.
  • If the phone is lost, act fast. Use the find-my-device feature to locate, lock, or erase the phone. Because passkeys need your fingerprint, face, or PIN to be used, a locked phone does not hand an attacker your accounts — but erasing it promptly removes all doubt.
  • Create a fresh passkey on your replacement phone. Once you have recovered the account, create a new passkey on the new device and remove the old device from your account's trusted devices list.

Everyday habits that keep passkeys safe

  • Only create passkeys on the real site. A passkey is tied to the genuine website, which protects you from phishing — but only create one when you arrived at the site yourself, not by clicking a link in a message.
  • Keep your devices updated. Passkey security depends on your device's secure hardware and software working together. Updates patch the weaknesses that could, in theory, undermine that protection.
  • Use a screen lock on every device that holds passkeys. A passkey is approved by your fingerprint, face, or PIN — a device with no lock screen removes that protection entirely.
  • Review your passkeys once a year. In your Google or Apple account settings, check which sites have passkeys and remove any for services you no longer use. Fewer stored credentials means fewer things to manage.
  • Do not share passkeys. Some password managers allow sharing a passkey with a family member. Only do this deliberately, for a shared account, and understand that the person you share with can then sign in as you.
  • Beware of fake "set up your passkey" messages. Scammers already send messages claiming your passkey needs verification. No legitimate service asks you to create or verify a passkey through a link in a message — always go to the site yourself.

Key Takeaways

  • No password to steal: A passkey is a key pair, not a shared secret — there is nothing to guess, leak, or phish.
  • Phishing-resistant by design: A passkey only works on the genuine website, so fake login pages cannot use it.
  • Start with your email: Set up a passkey on your Google or Apple account first, because it guards your other accounts.
  • Keep a backup way in: Keep your password and a second two-factor method until you are sure the passkey works everywhere you need it.
  • Sync is your safety net: Passkeys synced through your password manager survive a lost phone; device-only passkeys do not.
  • Recovery information matters: A current recovery email and phone number are what save you if everything else fails.
  • Stay alert for fake passkey messages: No real service asks you to set up a passkey through a link in a message.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — securing your own accounts, with no attempt to access anyone else's.
