How to Spot and Stop SMS Scams (Smishing): Protect Your Phone, Bank and SIM (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 19 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 13 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Your phone buzzes. A text message says your bank account will be blocked today unless you click a link and verify your details. Another says you have won a prize, or that your parcel is stuck at customs, or that your BISP payment is ready to collect. The message looks official, the sender name looks familiar, and the link looks real. This is smishing — phishing by SMS — and it is one of the fastest-growing scams in Pakistan. Unlike an email, which you can inspect calmly on a big screen, a text message arrives on the small screen you trust most, and it usually creates urgency: act now, or lose your money. Criminals send millions of these messages because they work. A single tapped link can install malware, steal your banking login, or hand over the one-time code that empties your JazzCash or Easypaisa wallet. The good news is that SMS scams follow a small number of scripts, and once you know them, they are easy to spot. This guide explains what smishing is, the exact SMS scams running in Pakistan right now, the warning signs, and the practical steps to protect your phone, your bank account and your SIM — plus exactly what to do if you already clicked a link or shared a code.

What is smishing?

Smishing is short for "SMS phishing". It is a text message that pretends to be from a trusted organisation — your bank, the government, a courier company, or a mobile network — and tries to trick you into clicking a link, calling a number, replying with information, or sharing a secret code. The message is the bait. What happens next depends on the scam:

  • The fake website. The link opens a page that looks exactly like your bank's login page, the NADRA website, or a courier tracking page. You type your account number, password, or card details, and the criminal captures them. This is the same trick as email phishing, delivered by text. Our phishing protection guide explains the fake-website trick in full.
  • The malware download. The link asks you to download an app — a "parcel tracking app", a "bank security update", or a "prize claim app". That app is malware. Once installed, it can read your SMS messages (including your bank codes), steal your contacts, and even take money from your mobile wallet. Our fake apps guide explains how lookalike apps steal your account.
  • The stolen code. The message asks you to share the one-time code (OTP) that was just sent to your phone — or a caller follows up by phone and asks for it. That code is the key to your bank account, your JazzCash or Easypaisa wallet, or your WhatsApp account. The moment you share it, the criminal logs in as you. Our phone scams guide explains how the follow-up call works.
  • The advance fee. The message says you won a prize, a grant, or a foreign remittance, but you must first pay a small "tax", "processing fee" or "delivery charge" — usually through JazzCash or Easypaisa. You pay, and the prize never comes. The fee is the whole scam.

Smishing works because text messages feel personal and urgent. We open almost every SMS we receive, often within minutes, and we rarely stop to check who really sent it. Criminals know this, and they send their messages in bulk — thousands at a time — because even a tiny success rate pays well.

The 7 SMS scams running in Pakistan right now

Most SMS scams in Pakistan are variations of the same seven scripts. If you can recognise the script, you can delete the message without a second thought:

  • 1. The fake bank message. "Dear customer, your account will be blocked today. Verify your details here:" followed by a link. Or: "Your debit card has been blocked due to suspicious activity. Call this number to unblock." Real banks never ask you to verify your account, update your details, or share your PIN through a link in a text message. If you are worried, close the message and call your bank on the number printed on the back of your card — or open your bank's official app yourself.
  • 2. The BISP and Ehsaas payment message. "Your BISP payment of Rs. 10,500 is ready. Click here to collect" or "Ehsaas program: you have been selected, send your CNIC number to claim." These messages target people waiting for government support, and they are always fake. BISP never sends payment links by SMS, and it never asks for your CNIC, PIN, or bank code by text. If you are a BISP beneficiary, check your payment only through the official 8171 service or the official BISP office. Never share your CNIC number with a number that texted you first.
  • 3. The parcel and courier message. "Your parcel is held at customs. Pay Rs. 450 to release it:" or "Delivery failed — update your address here:". These messages surge during online shopping seasons. Real couriers do not ask you to pay customs fees through a random link, and they do not need you to download a tracking app. If you are genuinely expecting a parcel, track it through the courier's official website or app — type the address yourself, never tap the link in the message.
  • 4. The prize, lottery and lucky draw message. "Congratulations! You have won a motorcycle / a cash prize / an Umrah package in the lucky draw. Pay a small processing fee to claim." You did not enter a draw, and real prizes never ask you to pay money to receive money. Any message that asks you to send money to receive money is a scam — every time, without exception.
  • 5. The fake job and online earning message. "Work from home, earn Rs. 50,000 per month. Send your details to apply" or "You are selected for a job — pay a registration fee to start." Real employers do not hire through random SMS, and they never ask for a registration fee. Our fake job offers guide explains the job-scam version of this trick in detail.
  • 6. The "wrong number" and family emergency message. "Assalam-o-Alaikum, I am your cousin, I changed my number. I am in trouble, send me money urgently." Or a message that says your son, brother, or husband is in an accident and needs money now. This is the SMS version of the romance and family scam. Before sending any money, call the person on their real, saved number. A thirty-second voice call stops this scam completely. Our romance scams guide explains how criminals build trust before asking for money.
  • 7. The fake tax, FBR and utility message. "Your electricity will be disconnected in 2 hours. Pay now:" or "FBR: your tax refund is ready, verify your bank details." Government departments and utility companies do not collect payments through SMS links. If you receive one, check your bill through the official app or website, or visit the office. Never pay from a link in a text message.

8 warning signs that a text message is a scam

You do not need to memorise every scam script. These eight signs are enough to catch almost all of them:

  • Urgency and threats. "Today only", "in 2 hours", "your account will be blocked", "your electricity will be cut". Scammers create panic because a frightened person does not think. Real banks and government offices give you time and send letters — they do not threaten you by text.
  • A link you did not expect. Any unexpected link in a text message should be treated as dangerous until proven otherwise. Even if the link looks like your bank's website, the real address may be hidden behind a shortened link or a lookalike spelling. Our guide to spotting fake websites shows how to check a link before you trust it.
  • An unknown or strange sender. The message comes from a normal mobile number (starting with 03xx) instead of an official short code, or from a name you do not recognise. Banks, BISP and couriers send messages from registered sender names or short codes — not from a personal-looking number. But be careful: criminals can also fake a sender name, so a familiar name alone is not proof a message is real.
  • Requests for secret information. Any message asking for your password, PIN, OTP, CNIC number, or card number is a scam. No real organisation asks for these by SMS. Your OTP is yours alone — even a real bank employee will never ask for it.
  • Spelling mistakes and strange wording. "Your acount has been bloked, verifi now." Criminals often write in a hurry or in a second language. Official messages from a bank or government department are written carefully. Mistakes are a strong warning sign — but a perfectly written message can still be fake, so do not rely on this sign alone.
  • A prize you never entered. You cannot win a draw you never joined. A prize, grant, or refund you were not expecting is not luck — it is bait.
  • A request to download an app. No bank, courier, or government office asks you to download an app from a link in a text message. Real apps come only from the Google Play Store or the Apple App Store.
  • A request for money to receive money. A "processing fee", "tax", "delivery charge" or "registration fee" paid before you receive a prize, a job, or a payment. Real prizes, jobs and government payments never work this way.

If a message shows even one of these signs, delete it. If it shows two or more, it is almost certainly a scam.

Step 1: Never click a link in a text message — verify another way

This is the single most important rule in this guide. A link in a text message can take you anywhere, and on a phone screen it is hard to tell a real website from a fake one. Instead of tapping the link, always verify through a channel you control:

  1. Open the official app yourself. If the message claims to be from your bank, close the message, open your bank's official app from your home screen, and check your account there. If the message is real, the same information will be in the app.
  2. Type the website address yourself. If you need to check a courier or a government service, open your browser and type the official address yourself — or use a bookmark you saved earlier. Never use the link the message gave you.
  3. Call the official number. Call your bank on the number printed on the back of your card, or the organisation on the number from its official website. Never call a number printed inside the suspicious message — that number belongs to the criminal.
  4. Check with the sender's official channel. For a parcel, check the tracking number on the courier's official site. For a government payment, check through the official helpline or office.

Think of it this way: a text message is a stranger knocking on your door and telling you to follow them somewhere. You would not follow a stranger. Check the claim yourself, through a door you trust.

Step 2: Never share an OTP, PIN or password — not even with "the bank"

Your one-time code (OTP) is the last line of defence for your bank account, your JazzCash or Easypaisa wallet, your WhatsApp account, and your email. Anyone who has your OTP can log in as you, change your password, and move your money. Remember these rules:

  • No real employee will ever ask for your OTP. Not your bank, not JazzCash, not Easypaisa, not WhatsApp, not the police, not NADRA. If anyone asks for your code — by text, by call, or in person — they are a criminal. Hang up, or stop replying.
  • Read the code message before you use it. A real OTP message tells you what the code is for: "Your JazzCash login code is 482913. Do not share it with anyone." If you receive a code you did not request, someone is trying to log in as you right now. Do not share it, and change your password immediately.
  • Never type a code into a website from a text link. A fake login page will ask for your OTP as the "second step" of stealing your account. If a website asks for a code you received by SMS, make sure you reached that website yourself — not through a link in a message.
  • Turn on app-based two-factor authentication where you can. An authenticator app is safer than SMS codes, because a criminal who tricks you into sharing an SMS code cannot steal a code from your authenticator app. Our two-factor authentication guide explains how to set this up for your Google, WhatsApp and banking accounts.

Step 3: Check the sender before you trust the message

Before you act on any text message, spend ten seconds checking who really sent it:

  • Look at the sender, not just the name. On most phones, tapping the sender's name shows the actual number or short code behind it. Official messages come from short codes (like 8171 for BISP) or registered sender names. A message from a normal 03xx mobile number claiming to be your bank is fake.
  • Remember that sender names can be faked. Criminals can make a message appear to come from "HBL", "JazzCash" or "BISP". A familiar sender name is not proof. The message still has to pass the other checks in this guide — no unexpected link, no request for codes, no urgency.
  • Compare with real messages you already have. Open an old, genuine message from your bank and compare the sender, the wording, and the style. Scam messages almost always look slightly different — a different sender name, a different short code, or a link where the real bank never puts one.
  • Search the message text. If you are unsure, search the exact wording of the message on Google. Scam messages are sent to thousands of people, and other people often report them online. If the same message appears in scam reports, delete it.

Step 4: Block, report and delete — in that order

When you receive a scam SMS, do these three things. It takes less than a minute, and it protects you and everyone else:

  1. Do not reply, do not click, do not call back. Replying tells the criminal your number is active, and they will send you more scams. Even replying "STOP" can confirm your number is real. Just leave the message alone.
  2. Report it. In Pakistan, you can report a scam SMS to the PTA by forwarding it to 9000. The PTA uses these reports to block the numbers behind the scams. You can also report the number inside your messaging app: on Android, open the message, tap the three dots, and choose "Block" and "Report spam"; on iPhone, tap the sender, then "Report Junk". Reporting takes seconds, and it helps shut the scam down for everyone.
  3. Block the number and delete the message. Blocking stops the same number messaging you again. Deleting removes the dangerous link from your phone so nobody in your family taps it by accident — especially children or elderly parents who use your phone.

If the message pretended to be from your bank, tell your bank too. Call the bank's official helpline and report the message. Banks track these scams, warn other customers, and can watch your account for suspicious activity.

Step 5: Protect your SIM, your bank and your wallet

Most SMS scams aim at your money, so lock down the three things they target:

  • Put a PIN on your SIM. If someone steals your phone, they can move your SIM into another phone and receive your bank codes. A SIM PIN stops that. On Android, go to Settings, then Security, then SIM card lock, and turn on "Lock SIM card". On iPhone, go to Settings, then Mobile Data (or Cellular), then SIM PIN. Choose a PIN that is not your birthday or 1234.
  • Turn on bank alerts. Ask your bank to send you an SMS or app notification for every transaction, even small ones. The moment money moves, you know. If you see a transaction you did not make, call your bank immediately — the faster you report it, the better the chance of stopping it.
  • Guard your JazzCash and Easypaisa PIN. Your mobile wallet PIN is as important as your bank card PIN. Never share it, never type it into a website or app from a link, and never tell it to a caller — even one who claims to be from JazzCash or Easypaisa. Keep your wallet app locked with a fingerprint or PIN, and never leave your wallet logged in on a phone you lend to someone else.
  • Use a separate, strong password for your bank and email. If a criminal gets your email password, they can reset your bank password too. Give every important account its own password, and store them in a password manager. Our password manager guide and our password security guide show how.
  • Keep your phone's software updated. Updates fix the security holes that malware uses. Turn on automatic updates for your phone, and update your banking and wallet apps from the Play Store or App Store. Our malware removal guide explains what to do if your phone is already infected.

Our SIM swapping protection guide explains a related attack — where a criminal takes over your number entirely — and how to stop it.

Step 6: If you already clicked the link or shared a code — act now

It happens to careful people too. If you clicked a link in a scam message, or shared a code, do not panic — act quickly, in this order:

  1. Call your bank immediately on the number on the back of your card. Tell them you may have shared your details with a scammer. Ask them to freeze your account or card, and to watch for suspicious transactions. If money has already moved, ask what their fraud process is and file a complaint the same day.
  2. Change your passwords now. Change the password of the account the message targeted — your bank, your email, your JazzCash or Easypaisa account — and any other account that used the same password. Do it from your official app or by typing the website address yourself, never from a link in a message.
  3. Check where you are logged in. Open your bank app, your email, and your WhatsApp, and check the active sessions or logged-in devices. Log out any device you do not recognise. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the wider checks for your whole phone.
  4. If you downloaded an app from the link, delete it immediately. Go to your phone settings, find the app, and uninstall it. Then run a scan with Google Play Protect (Play Store, then your profile picture, then Play Protect) or your phone's built-in security scan. If the phone keeps behaving strangely — pop-ups, a hot battery, messages you did not send — back up your photos and contacts, and reset the phone to factory settings.
  5. Warn your contacts. If the criminal got into your WhatsApp or your contacts, they may message your family pretending to be you. Post a warning and tell your family directly: "Do not send money or click links from my number today."
  6. Report the crime. In Pakistan, SMS fraud and the theft that follows it can be reported to the FIA Cybercrime Wing. Keep screenshots of the message, the link, the number that sent it, and any money-transfer details. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.

The first hour matters most. A criminal who has your code will use it immediately, so every minute you spend worrying is a minute they spend moving your money. Call the bank first, then fix the rest.

What real banks and the government never do by SMS

Keep this list on your phone, or teach it to your family. If a message does any of these, it is fake:

  • A bank never asks you to click a link to verify your account, update your details, or unblock your card.
  • A bank never asks for your PIN, password, OTP, or card number by text, call, or email.
  • BISP never sends payment links by SMS and never asks for your CNIC or PIN by text.
  • A courier never asks you to pay customs fees or download a tracking app through an SMS link.
  • FBR and utility companies never collect payments through links in text messages.
  • No organisation gives you a prize, a job, or a grant and asks you to pay a fee first.

Teach this list to your parents, your children, and anyone in your family who uses a phone. The people scammers target most are the ones nobody warned — and a five-minute conversation with your family can save them from losing their savings.

Key Takeaways

  • Smishing is phishing by text message — a fake message from a "bank", the government, or a courier, designed to steal your login, your code, or your money.
  • Never click a link in a text message. Open the official app yourself, type the website address yourself, or call the official number.
  • Never share an OTP, PIN, or password with anyone — no real employee of any bank, wallet, or government office will ever ask for it.
  • Watch for the warning signs: urgency and threats, an unexpected link, a strange sender, a request for secret information, a prize you never entered, or a fee to receive money.
  • Report scam SMS to the PTA by forwarding them to 9000, then block the number and delete the message.
  • Protect your SIM with a PIN, turn on bank alerts, and guard your JazzCash and Easypaisa PIN like your bank card PIN.
  • If you clicked or shared a code, call your bank immediately, change your passwords, check your sessions, and report it to the FIA.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your own phone, your own money, and your own family from scammers.
