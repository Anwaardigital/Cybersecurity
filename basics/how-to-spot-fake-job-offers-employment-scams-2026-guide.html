How to Spot Fake Job Offers and Employment Scams Online (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 23 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 13 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

For many people in Pakistan, a message about a job feels like hope — a chance to earn from home, support a family, or start a career. Criminals know this, and fake job offers are now one of the most common online scams in the country. The message usually arrives on WhatsApp, Facebook, or SMS: "Work from home, earn Rs. 50,000 per month, no experience needed", "Urgent hiring — data entry, typing work, part-time", or "You have been selected for a job in Dubai — send your documents and pay the visa fee". The job does not exist. The real goal is your money, your identity documents, or your bank account. Some victims lose a "registration fee" of a few thousand rupees. Others lose much more: their CNIC copy, their bank details, or their WhatsApp account itself. The good news is that fake job offers follow a small set of patterns, and once you know them, they are easy to spot. This guide explains how job scams actually work, the 10 red flags that give them away, how to verify any company in five minutes, what a real employer never asks for, and exactly what to do if you already shared your details or paid a fee. Everything here is defensive: protecting yourself, your family, and your money from employment fraud.

How fake job scams actually work

Job scams are not one trick — they are several tricks wearing the same disguise. These are the versions reported most often in Pakistan:

  • The upfront-fee trap (the most common). You apply, you are "selected" within hours, and then comes the fee: a "registration fee", "training fee", "document verification fee", or "visa processing fee" — usually Rs. 2,000 to Rs. 20,000, payable by JazzCash or Easypaisa. You pay, and then there is always one more fee, until you stop paying — and then the "employer" disappears. A real employer never charges you to hire you. Our fake job offers guide explains this classic version of the trick.
  • The WhatsApp/Telegram "interview". The whole hiring process happens in a chat app. There is no office, no phone call from a company landline, no official email — just a person on WhatsApp asking for your CV, your CNIC, and your bank details. Real companies interview on a video call or in an office, from a company email address, and they do not hire you in a chat message.
  • The data-harvesting job. The "job" is just a reason to collect your personal information: a copy of your CNIC, your photo, your bank account number, your mother's name, your address. That information is then used for identity theft, to open accounts in your name, or to target you with more scams. A real employer only asks for sensitive documents after a formal offer, in person or through a verified company channel — never in the first chat message.
  • The task scam ("like and earn" / "rate products"). You are asked to complete small online tasks — liking videos, rating products, writing reviews — and you are paid a small amount at first. Then you are told to "invest" or "deposit" money to unlock bigger tasks and bigger earnings. The first small payment is bait. Once you deposit, your money is gone. This is the same pattern as the crypto and "double your money" schemes in our brand deal scams guide.
  • The money-mule / reshipping job. You are hired to receive money or parcels and forward them to someone else, keeping a "commission". In reality, you are moving stolen money or goods for criminals — and when the police investigate, the trail leads to you. Never accept a job that involves receiving money or parcels and sending them on to strangers.
  • The fake overseas job. A "recruitment agent" promises a job in the Gulf, Europe, or Canada, shows you a fake visa or work permit, and charges a large "processing fee". Real overseas jobs go through licensed overseas employment promoters registered with the Bureau of Emigration and Overseas Employment in Pakistan, and you can verify the promoter's licence before paying anything.

10 red flags of a fake job offer

If you see even two or three of these, stop and verify before you reply, send any document, or pay anything:

  • 1. You have to pay to get the job. Registration fee, training fee, uniform fee, security deposit, visa fee — any payment before you start work is a scam. Real employers pay you; they never charge you.
  • 2. The pay is far too good for the work. "Rs. 80,000 per month for 2 hours of typing work, no experience needed" is not a job — it is bait. Compare the salary with similar real jobs on trusted job sites. If it is double or triple the normal rate for easy work, it is fake.
  • 3. The job description is vague. "Online work", "data entry", "typing job", "work from home" — with no company name, no office address, no clear duties, and no named manager. Real job ads say what the company does, what you will do, and where you will do it.
  • 4. You are hired too fast. You applied an hour ago and you are already "selected" — with no interview, no test, and no reference check. Real hiring takes days or weeks, even for simple jobs.
  • 5. The contact is only on WhatsApp or Telegram. No company website, no company email address, no office phone number — just a personal mobile number and a chat app. Real companies have a website, a company email domain, and a landline you can call.
  • 6. The email address is free or slightly wrong. The "HR manager" writes from a Gmail or Yahoo address, or from a domain that is almost the company name but not quite — "hr-jazzcareers.com" instead of jazz.com.pk, or "nestle-jobs.org" instead of nestle.pk. Our fake website guide explains how to check a domain.
  • 7. They ask for sensitive information too early. Your CNIC number and copy, your bank account details, your passwords, your OTP, or a photo of your debit card — before any interview or offer letter. A real employer asks for documents only after a formal offer, and never asks for your password or OTP. Ever.
  • 8. The message creates urgency. "Only 2 seats left", "Apply today or the offer expires", "Pay now or your selection is cancelled". Scammers rush you because a person in a hurry does not check. Real employers give you days to decide.
  • 9. Bad English, copied logos, and stolen photos. The job ad has spelling mistakes, a blurry or stretched company logo, and photos copied from the internet. Check the company's real website and compare — the real logo and the real job page will look different.
  • 10. The offer letter looks wrong. A real offer letter is on company letterhead, has a reference number, a named signatory, a company stamp, and the company's real address and phone number. A fake one is a plain PDF or a WhatsApp image with spelling mistakes and no verifiable details.

If several of these apply, treat the offer as fake until proven otherwise — not the other way round. The burden of proof is on the employer, not on you.

Step 1: Verify the company in five minutes

Before you reply to any job offer, run these five checks. They take about five minutes, and they catch most scams:

  1. Search the company name + "scam" or "fraud". Type the company name into Google followed by "scam", "fraud", or "fake". Victims of the same scam often post warnings on Facebook groups, Reddit, or review sites. Also search the phone number or WhatsApp number that contacted you — scammers reuse numbers.
  2. Visit the company's real website yourself. Do not click the link in the message. Type the company name into Google, open the official website, and check the careers or jobs page. If the job is real, it is usually listed there. If the company has no website at all, that is itself a red flag.
  3. Check the company on LinkedIn. A real company usually has a LinkedIn page with employees, a logo, and a history. A company with no LinkedIn presence, no employees, and no history is suspicious. You can also check whether the "HR manager" who messaged you actually works there.
  4. Call the company on its official number. Find the number on the company's official website — not the number in the job message — and call to ask: "Is this job real? Is this person your employee?" A five-minute call can save you thousands of rupees.
  5. For overseas jobs, check the licence. In Pakistan, overseas recruitment must go through a licensed Overseas Employment Promoter. Check the promoter's name and licence on the Bureau of Emigration and Overseas Employment website before paying any fee, and never pay a "visa fee" to a personal JazzCash or Easypaisa number.

Step 2: Check the email, the domain, and the job ad itself

Most fake job offers fail a careful reading. Here is what to check:

  • The sender's email domain. A real recruiter writes from the company's own domain — for example, careers@company.com.pk. A Gmail, Yahoo, or Hotmail address for a "big company" is a warning sign. Also watch for lookalike domains: one letter changed, an extra word added, or ".com" swapped for ".org". Our phone scams guide explains the same lookalike trick used in calls and messages.
  • The job ad's details. A real job ad names the company, the job title, the location, the duties, the required experience, and the salary range. A fake ad is vague about all of these and generous about only one thing: the salary.
  • The website the ad links to. Hover over the link (do not click it) and look at the address. If it is a free website builder address, a shortened link, or a domain that was registered very recently, be suspicious. Our fake website guide shows how to check a website before trusting it.
  • Reverse-search the logo and photos. Save the company logo or the "office photo" from the ad and search it on Google Images. Scammers steal logos and office photos from real companies. If the same photo appears on ten unrelated websites, it is stolen.
  • Compare the salary with the market. Search the same job title on a trusted job site such as Rozee.pk or LinkedIn. If real employers pay Rs. 30,000–40,000 for that work and this offer promises Rs. 100,000, it is not a generous employer — it is a trap.

Step 3: The interview test — what real employers never do

A real hiring process has a shape. If the process breaks that shape, walk away:

  • Real employers interview you properly — on a video call where you can see the interviewer, or in an office you can visit. A "text interview" on WhatsApp, where you just answer questions in chat, is not a real interview.
  • Real employers never ask for money. Not for registration, training, uniforms, a laptop, a security deposit, or a visa. If anyone asks you to pay before you start work, it is a scam — no exceptions.
  • Real employers never ask for your password, your OTP, or your bank login. They may ask for your bank account number later, to pay your salary — after a signed offer, through official channels. They never ask for your password or the code from your phone.
  • Real employers give you a written offer on letterhead with a reference number, a start date, a salary, and a contact person — and you can verify it by calling the company's official number. If the "offer letter" is a WhatsApp photo with no verifiable details, it is fake.
  • Real employers give you time. You get days to read the offer, ask questions, and decide. Pressure to decide in hours, or to pay today, is a scammer's tool.

Step 4: Protect your documents and your accounts

Even if a job offer turns out to be real, protect your information until you are sure:

  • Never send your CNIC copy, your passport copy, or your bank details in the first message. Share documents only after a verified offer, and only through the company's official email or office — never to a personal WhatsApp number.
  • Watermark document copies you send. When you do send a CNIC copy for a real application, write across it (or ask the employer to accept a copy marked) "For job application at [company name] only, [date]". A marked copy is much harder for a criminal to reuse for identity theft.
  • Use a separate email address for job hunting. Create one email address just for job applications. If it starts receiving scam messages, your main email — and the accounts linked to it — stay safe. Our password security guide and two-factor authentication guide explain how to protect that email account.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere. Your email, your WhatsApp, your Facebook — if a scammer gets your password, two-factor authentication is what stops them getting into your accounts. Our two-factor authentication guide shows how to set it up.
  • Never install an app or open a file a "recruiter" sends you. "Install this app to complete your application" or "Open this form to register" is often malware that steals your passwords and your contacts. Our fake apps guide explains how lookalike apps steal your account.

You already shared your details or paid — act now

If you already sent money or documents to a fake employer, do not feel ashamed — these scams are designed to fool careful people. Act fast, because the first hours matter most:

  1. If you paid money, contact your bank or wallet immediately. Call your bank's helpline or the JazzCash/Easypaisa helpline and report the transfer as fraud. Ask whether the transaction can be reversed or the recipient's account frozen. The sooner you report, the better the chance of recovery.
  2. If you shared your CNIC copy, watch for identity theft. Check your bank accounts and mobile wallets daily for transactions you did not make. Consider telling your bank that your CNIC copy was shared, and ask what protections they can add to your account.
  3. If you shared your bank details, change your passwords and PINs now. Change your online banking password, your email password, and your ATM PIN. Turn on two-factor authentication on your email and banking apps. Our data breach recovery guide explains the full recovery steps.
  4. If you shared a password or OTP, change that password everywhere you used it. If you use the same password on other accounts, change it on all of them — a scammer will try it everywhere. Our password security guide explains how to build strong, unique passwords.
  5. If you installed an app or opened a file they sent, remove it. Uninstall the app, run a scan with a trusted security app, and check your phone for new apps or changed settings. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the warning signs to look for.
  6. Keep all the evidence. Save screenshots of the job ad, the chat, the payment receipt, the phone numbers, and the offer letter. Do not delete anything — you will need it for the report.
  7. Warn others. Post the scammer's number and the fake job ad in your family and friends groups, and on the job platform where you found it. One warning can save the next person.

How to report a fake job offer in Pakistan

Reporting helps the police and the platforms stop the same scammer from targeting the next person. Keep your screenshots and payment receipts ready before you report:

  • FIA Cybercrime Wing. Report online employment fraud to the FIA Cybercrime Wing — online through their complaint portal, or at your nearest FIA Cybercrime Reporting Centre. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.
  • The platform where you found the job. Report the fake ad on the job site, Facebook, WhatsApp, or Telegram. Reporting gets the ad removed and the account banned, so it cannot reach more victims.
  • PTA, for SMS scams. If the fake job offer came by SMS, forward it to 668 — the PTA's short code for reporting spam and scam messages.
  • Your bank or wallet provider. Report the recipient's JazzCash, Easypaisa, or bank account as a fraud account. Banks and wallet companies can flag and block accounts that receive fraud reports.
  • The real company, if its name was used. If a scammer used a real company's name, tell the company. Many companies post public warnings about fake job offers using their name, and they can get the fake accounts removed.

Key Takeaways

  • Never pay to get a job — no registration fee, training fee, or visa fee. Real employers pay you; they never charge you.
  • If the pay is far too good for easy work, it is bait — compare the salary with real jobs for the same work.
  • A real interview is a video call or an office visit — not a text chat on WhatsApp or Telegram.
  • Verify the company in five minutes — search its name + "scam", check its real website and LinkedIn, and call its official number.
  • Check the email domain — a real recruiter writes from the company's own domain, not Gmail or a lookalike address.
  • Never share your CNIC copy, bank details, password, or OTP in the first message — and watermark any document copy you do send.
  • Never install an app or open a file a "recruiter" sends you — it is often malware.
  • If you already paid or shared details, act fast — call your bank or wallet, change your passwords, keep the evidence, and report to the FIA Cybercrime Wing.
  • Warn others — one message in your family group can save the next person from the same scam.
