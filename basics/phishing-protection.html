Phishing Protection: How to Spot Fake Emails, SMS & Calls

By CyberSec Hub · 11 min read

Phishing is the most successful cyberattack in history — not because the technology is advanced, but because it hacks you. A convincing fake message, a moment of hurry or fear, and credentials are gone. The good news: phishing follows patterns, and once you learn them, fakes become obvious.

The Three Flavors of Phishing

  • Phishing (email): fake emails impersonating banks, delivery companies, government, or colleagues.
  • Smishing (SMS): text messages about parcels, bank alerts, or "prizes" with malicious links.
  • Vishing (voice): phone calls from fake "bank officers," "police," or "tech support" demanding info or payments.

All three use the same psychological weapons: urgency, fear, authority, and curiosity.

Seven Red Flags of a Phishing Message

  1. Urgency and threats: "Your account will be suspended in 24 hours!" Real companies do not communicate like this.
  2. Generic greetings: "Dear customer" instead of your name. Your real bank knows your name.
  3. Suspicious sender: support@secure-bankk-login.com is not your bank. Check the full address, not just the display name.
  4. Unexpected attachments or links: invoices, "statements," or tracking links you never asked for.
  5. Too good to be true: lottery wins, inheritances, unbelievable discounts.
  6. Requests for sensitive info: no legitimate company asks for your password, OTP, or PIN by email or call.
  7. Poor language: many (not all) phishing messages have odd grammar or formatting.

The 10-Second Verification Routine

Before clicking anything in a surprising message, pause and do this:

  1. Stop. Urgency is the weapon — refuse to hurry.
  2. Check the sender — full email address or number, not the display name.
  3. Hover, don't click — on desktop, hover over links to see the real destination.
  4. Go direct — open the official app or type the official website yourself instead of using the message's link.
  5. Call back officially — if "your bank" calls, hang up and call the number on your card.
💡 The OTP rule: a one-time password is called one-time because only the real service should ever receive it. Anyone who calls or messages asking for your OTP is a scammer — always, no exceptions.

Common Phishing Scenarios in Pakistan & Beyond

  • Fake parcel SMS: "Your TCS/Leopards parcel is held — pay Rs. 150 at this link." Delivery companies never work this way.
  • Bank "verification" calls: callers claiming to be from your bank asking for card numbers or OTPs.
  • WhatsApp hijacking: "I sent you a code by mistake, please forward it" — that code is your WhatsApp verification; forwarding it hands over your account.
  • Job and prize scams: upfront "fees" for jobs or winnings that never existed.
  • Fake tech support: popups or calls claiming your device is infected, then demanding remote access or payment.

What To Do If You Clicked

  1. Disconnect from the internet if you downloaded something suspicious.
  2. Change the password of the affected account from a clean device, and anywhere you reused it.
  3. Enable 2FA immediately.
  4. Check bank statements for unfamiliar transactions and alert your bank.
  5. Report the message: mark as phishing in your email app, forward SMS spam to your carrier's reporting number, and warn the impersonated company.

Protect Your Family Too

Elderly relatives and young teens are prime targets. Share the 10-second routine with them, help them enable 2FA, and agree on a family rule: never share OTPs with anyone, ever. Also see our mobile security guide for locking down the device where most phishing lands.

Advanced Phishing: What Experts Watch For

Basic phishing is easy to spot; advanced campaigns are not. Spear phishing targets you personally, using details from your social media — your boss's name, your recent purchase, your university. Business email compromise (BEC) impersonates executives to trick finance staff into wiring money; these emails are often plain text with zero links, defeating technical filters. QR code phishing (quishing) hides malicious links in QR codes on fake parking tickets or restaurant menus. The defense is the same in every case: verify through an independent channel before acting on any unexpected request involving money, credentials, or access.

Technical Defenses That Help

  • Email authentication (SPF, DKIM, DMARC): standards that let your email provider reject forged senders — ask your IT team or email host whether these are enforced on your domain.
  • Browser warnings: modern browsers flag known phishing sites — never click through those warnings.
  • Link preview: on mobile, long-press links to preview the real destination before opening.
  • Separate email addresses: use one address for banking/important accounts and another for newsletters and signups — phishing to the wrong address is instantly suspicious.

Teaching Kids About Phishing

Children get phishing too — fake game currency offers, "free Robux" scams, and messages from "friends" whose accounts were hijacked. Teach them: never click links promising free game items, never share account details even with "friends" online (verify in person or by voice call), and always show a parent unexpected messages asking for anything. Make it a no-blame rule — kids who fear punishment hide incidents instead of reporting them.
