How to Spot and Avoid Fake Prize and Lottery Scams (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 24 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 13 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Your phone buzzes with a message: "Congratulations! Your mobile number has won Rs. 500,000 in the lucky draw. Call now to claim your prize." Or a call comes in: "You have won a brand-new car in our lottery — just pay a small delivery fee and it is yours." For a few seconds, it feels wonderful. That is exactly the feeling the scammer is counting on. Fake prize and lottery scams are among the oldest tricks on the internet — and they still work every single day in Pakistan, because they run on excitement, not logic. The prize does not exist. There was no draw, no lottery, and no lucky number. The real goal is simple: to get you to pay a "fee" or "tax" to claim a prize that will never arrive, or to steal your personal information and your bank details while you are distracted by the good news. Some victims lose a few thousand rupees in "processing fees". Others lose far more: their CNIC copy, their bank account details, their WhatsApp account, or even their life savings after a chain of "one more fee" payments. The good news is that prize scams follow a small set of patterns, and once you know them, they are easy to spot. This guide explains how fake prize and lottery scams actually work, the 10 red flags that give them away, how to verify any prize in five minutes, what a real lottery or giveaway never asks for, and exactly what to do if you already paid a fee or shared your details. Everything here is defensive: protecting yourself, your family, and your money from prize fraud.

How fake prize and lottery scams actually work

Prize scams are not one trick — they are several tricks wearing the same disguise. These are the versions reported most often in Pakistan:

  • The "you won" SMS or WhatsApp message. A message arrives out of nowhere: "Congratulations! You have won Rs. 200,000 / a car / an iPhone in the lucky draw." You never entered any draw — that is the first clue. The message asks you to call a number or click a link to claim your prize. Our guide to SMS scams (smishing) explains how these messages are sent in bulk to thousands of numbers at once. The scammer only needs a handful of people to reply.
  • The advance-fee trap (the most common). You call the number, and a friendly "officer" confirms your win — then comes the fee: a "processing fee", "tax", "delivery charge", "customs duty", or "account activation fee", usually Rs. 2,000 to Rs. 50,000, payable by JazzCash or Easypaisa. You pay, and then there is always one more fee: the "tax" becomes a "customs clearance", then a "bank verification charge", then a "final release fee". Each payment is presented as the last one. Victims often pay several times before they realise the prize will never come. A real prize never asks you to pay money to receive it.
  • The fake celebrity or brand giveaway. A Facebook page, Instagram account, or YouTube channel that looks like a famous celebrity, a cricket star, a mobile company, or a bank announces a giveaway: "Like, share, and comment to win a car / a phone / cash." The page is a copy — the real celebrity has no connection to it. Winners are asked to pay a "delivery fee" or to share their bank details. Some of these fake pages have tens of thousands of followers, bought or stolen, which makes them look real. Our fake website guide explains the same lookalike trick used in fake pages and links.
  • The "claim your prize" phishing link. The message contains a link to a website that looks like a lottery, a bank, or a government scheme. The site asks you to "verify" yourself by entering your name, CNIC number, bank account, and even your ATM PIN or the OTP from your phone. Every detail you type goes straight to the criminal. Never enter bank details or an OTP on a website you reached from a prize message.
  • The phone call from a "lottery office". A caller claims to be from a lottery board, a telecom company, or a government department, and says your number was selected in a draw. They sound official, they may even play hold music, and they may know your name and city — information that is easy to buy or guess. Then comes the fee, or a request for your bank details "to transfer the prize". A real lottery never calls winners and asks for money or bank details over the phone. Our phone scams guide covers exactly how these calls work.
  • The fake courier or customs fee. You are told your prize — a phone, a laptop, a car — is waiting at a courier office or at customs, and you must pay a "delivery fee" or "customs duty" before it can be released. Sometimes a fake tracking number and a fake courier website are provided to make it look real. Real courier companies do not ask you to pay a random person on JazzCash to release a parcel, and real customs duty is never collected this way.
  • The "BISP / government scheme" version. The message claims you have won money under a government programme — BISP, Ehsaas, or a "Prime Minister's lucky draw" — and asks you to send your CNIC number or pay a "registration fee". Government programmes do not select winners by random SMS, do not ask for fees by JazzCash, and never ask for your OTP. If you are registered for a real programme, check your status only through the official channel you already know.

10 red flags of a fake prize or lottery

If you see even two or three of these, stop and verify before you reply, call back, click a link, or pay anything:

  • 1. You never entered the draw. This is the biggest red flag of all. You cannot win a lottery you never entered. If you did not buy a ticket, fill in a form, or join a competition, you did not win anything — the message is fake by definition.
  • 2. You have to pay to receive the prize. Processing fee, tax, delivery charge, customs duty, account activation — any payment before you receive the prize is a scam. A real prize is free. The winner never pays to collect it.
  • 3. The prize is far too big for a random draw. "You won a car / Rs. 1,000,000 / a house" — from a draw you never heard of, run by a company you cannot name. Huge prizes from unknown organisers are bait, not luck.
  • 4. The message creates urgency. "Claim within 2 hours or the prize goes to someone else", "Call now or your prize will be cancelled", "Only today". Scammers rush you because a person in a hurry does not check. A real prize does not expire in two hours.
  • 5. The contact is a personal mobile number or a chat app. No office address, no company landline, no official email — just a mobile number on WhatsApp, or a call from an unknown number. Real lotteries and real brands have offices, websites, and official channels you can verify.
  • 6. They ask for sensitive information. Your CNIC number and copy, your bank account number, your ATM PIN, your OTP, or a photo of your debit card — before you have even seen the prize. A real prize organiser never asks for your password, your PIN, or your OTP. Ever.
  • 7. The message is full of mistakes or strange details. Bad spelling, a blurry logo, a company name that is almost — but not quite — a real brand, or a website address that does not match the brand. Check the real brand's website and compare.
  • 8. The "proof" is a photo or a video that proves nothing. A screenshot of a "winner list" with your number on it, a photo of a pile of cash, a video of someone holding a prize — all of these are easy to fake or steal from the internet. A screenshot is not proof.
  • 9. They ask you to keep it secret. "Do not tell anyone about your prize until you receive it", "This is confidential". Scammers say this because a friend or family member would immediately tell you it is a scam. A real prize has no reason to be secret.
  • 10. The fee keeps growing. You paid the "processing fee", and now there is a "tax", then a "customs fee", then a "final charge". Each payment is always the last one — until the next one. This is the classic advance-fee pattern. Stop paying. The prize does not exist.

If several of these apply, treat the prize as fake until proven otherwise — not the other way round. The burden of proof is on the organiser, not on you.

Step 1: Verify the prize in five minutes

Before you reply to any prize message, run these five checks. They take about five minutes, and they catch most scams:

  1. Ask yourself: did I enter this? If you did not buy a ticket, enter a competition, or sign up for a draw, the message is fake. This one question ends most prize scams on the spot. No entry, no win — it is that simple.
  2. Search the exact message text on Google. Copy a sentence from the message — for example, the prize amount and the company name — and search it. Scammers send the same message to thousands of people, and victims often post warnings on Facebook groups, Reddit, or review sites. Also search the phone number that contacted you: scammers reuse numbers, and a quick search often shows other people reporting the same number.
  3. Find the real organisation yourself. Do not click the link in the message or call the number in the message. Instead, search for the company or brand name on Google, open its official website, and look for the competition or giveaway. If the prize is real, it is listed there. If the brand has no mention of any draw, the message is fake. Call the company on the number listed on its official website — not the number in the message — and ask whether the prize is real.
  4. Check the social media page properly. A fake celebrity or brand page often has a slightly different name, a recently created date, very few real posts, and comments disabled or full of fake "I won!" comments from empty accounts. Compare it with the verified account of the real celebrity or brand. A blue tick can also be faked in screenshots, so check the page itself — not a screenshot of it.
  5. Check the website address before you trust it. Hover over the link (do not click it) and look at the address. If it is a free website address, a shortened link, or a domain that is almost the brand name but not quite — one letter changed, an extra word added, ".com" swapped for ".org" or ".xyz" — be suspicious. Our fake website guide shows how to check a website before trusting it, and our SMS scams guide explains why links in prize messages are so dangerous.

Step 2: Check the message, the link, and the "proof"

Most fake prize messages fail a careful reading. Here is what to check:

  • The sender. A real brand or lottery writes from an official short code, a verified account, or its own domain. A prize message from a random mobile number, a personal WhatsApp account, or a Gmail address is a warning sign. Government departments do not announce prizes from personal numbers.
  • The language of the message. Prize scams often use urgent, emotional language: "Congratulations!!!", "You are the lucky winner!!!", "Act fast!!!". They also often contain spelling mistakes, mixed languages, and strange formatting. Real organisations write carefully and professionally.
  • The link. Never click a link in a prize message to "claim" your prize. If you want to check, type the organisation's real website address into your browser yourself. A link that asks you to log in, enter your CNIC, or enter an OTP is a phishing page — its only job is to steal what you type.
  • The "winner list" or certificate. Scammers send a photo of a list with your number on it, or a "winning certificate" with your name typed on it. Both take minutes to fake with a simple image editor. A certificate that arrives as a WhatsApp photo, with no way to verify it on an official website, proves nothing.
  • The fee request. Read the message again and look for the catch: a fee, a tax, a delivery charge, or a request for your bank details. A real prize announcement tells you what you won and how to collect it — it does not ask you to pay first or to share your OTP.
  • Reverse-search the photos. Save the prize photo, the "office photo", or the celebrity photo from the message and search it on Google Images. Scammers steal photos of cash, cars, and celebrities from the internet. If the same photo appears on ten unrelated websites, it is stolen.

Step 3: What a real prize or lottery never does

Real lotteries, real brands, and real giveaways have a shape. If the process breaks that shape, walk away:

  • Real prizes never ask you to pay first. Not a processing fee, not a tax, not a delivery charge, not a customs duty. If anyone asks you to pay before you receive the prize, it is a scam — no exceptions. Real winners pay nothing.
  • Real lotteries never ask for your password, your PIN, or your OTP. They may ask for your bank account number later, to transfer the prize — after you have verified them through official channels. They never ask for your password or the code from your phone, and they never ask you to share an OTP "to confirm your identity".
  • Real winners are contacted through official channels. A real brand contacts winners from a verified account or an official email address, and the prize is listed publicly on its real website. A "you won" message from a personal WhatsApp number is not an official channel.
  • Real prizes give you time. You get days or weeks to claim a prize, not two hours. Pressure to decide now, pay now, or click now is a scammer's tool. A deadline of a few hours is designed to stop you from checking.
  • Real lotteries do not ask you to keep the prize secret. A real winner can tell their family, and a real brand is happy to announce winners publicly. Secrecy only helps the scammer — because the first person you told would warn you.
  • Real prizes do not arrive through a random JazzCash or Easypaisa payment. If you are asked to send money to a personal mobile wallet to "release" your prize, that money is gone. Real fees, if any exist, are handled through official, traceable channels — and a genuine prize has no fee at all.

Step 4: Protect your money, your accounts, and your documents

Even if a prize message turns out to be real, protect your information until you are sure:

  • Never send your CNIC copy, your passport copy, or your bank details to claim a prize. Share documents only after you have verified the organiser through its official website and official phone number — never to a personal WhatsApp number that messaged you first.
  • Never share your OTP, your ATM PIN, or your online banking password with anyone. No prize, no bank, and no government department will ever ask for these. Anyone who asks is a criminal. Our password security guide explains how to build strong, unique passwords, and our two-factor authentication guide shows how to lock your accounts.
  • Never click links in prize messages. A single click can open a fake website that steals your login, or download malware onto your phone. If you want to check a prize, type the organisation's real website address into your browser yourself. Our fake apps guide explains how lookalike apps and links steal your account.
  • Use a separate email address for competitions and giveaways. If you do enter real competitions, use one email address just for them. If it starts receiving scam messages, your main email — and the accounts linked to it — stay safe.
  • Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere. Your email, your WhatsApp, your Facebook, your banking apps — if a scammer gets your password, two-factor authentication is what stops them getting into your accounts.
  • Talk to your family about prize scams — especially older relatives. Scammers target people who are less familiar with the internet, and older family members are often the first to receive these messages. One short conversation — "If a message says you won something, call me before you reply" — can save a family's savings. Our family digital safety plan shows how to protect everyone at home together.
  • Teach children the same rule. Children see giveaway posts on YouTube, TikTok, and gaming apps every day. Teach them that real prizes are never won by clicking a link in a comment, and that they should always ask an adult first. Our kids online safety guide covers this in detail.

You already paid or shared your details — act now

If you already sent money or documents to a fake prize scheme, do not feel ashamed — these scams are designed to fool careful people, and they work because they use excitement, not stupidity. Act fast, because the first hours matter most:

  1. If you paid money, contact your bank or wallet immediately. Call your bank's helpline or the JazzCash/Easypaisa helpline and report the transfer as fraud. Ask whether the transaction can be reversed or the recipient's account frozen. The sooner you report, the better the chance of recovery. Do not pay any more "fees" — no matter what the scammer promises, there is no prize, and every further payment is another loss.
  2. If you shared your CNIC copy, watch for identity theft. Check your bank accounts and mobile wallets daily for transactions you did not make. Tell your bank that your CNIC copy was shared, and ask what protections they can add to your account. Our identity theft protection guide explains the full steps.
  3. If you shared your bank details, change your passwords and PINs now. Change your online banking password, your email password, and your ATM PIN. Turn on two-factor authentication on your email and banking apps. Our data breach recovery guide explains the full recovery steps.
  4. If you shared a password or OTP, change that password everywhere you used it. If you use the same password on other accounts, change it on all of them — a scammer will try it everywhere. Our password security guide explains how to build strong, unique passwords.
  5. If you clicked a link or installed an app they sent, remove it. Uninstall the app, run a scan with a trusted security app, and check your phone for new apps or changed settings. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the warning signs to look for.
  6. Keep all the evidence. Save screenshots of the prize message, the chat, the payment receipt, the phone numbers, the fake website address, and any "certificate" they sent. Do not delete anything — you will need it for the report.
  7. Warn others. Post the scammer's number and the fake prize message in your family and friends groups, and report the fake page or account on the platform where you found it. One warning can save the next person.

How to report a fake prize or lottery scam in Pakistan

Reporting helps the police and the platforms stop the same scammer from targeting the next person. Keep your screenshots and payment receipts ready before you report:

  • FIA Cybercrime Wing. Report prize and lottery fraud to the FIA Cybercrime Wing — online through their complaint portal, or at your nearest FIA Cybercrime Reporting Centre. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.
  • The platform where you found the scam. Report the fake page, fake account, or fake ad on Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Telegram, or YouTube. Reporting gets the page removed and the account banned, so it cannot reach more victims.
  • PTA, for SMS scams. If the fake prize message came by SMS, forward it to 668 — the PTA's short code for reporting spam and scam messages.
  • Your bank or wallet provider. Report the recipient's JazzCash, Easypaisa, or bank account as a fraud account. Banks and wallet companies can flag and block accounts that receive fraud reports.
  • The real brand, if its name was used. If a scammer used a real company's or celebrity's name, tell the company. Many brands post public warnings about fake giveaways using their name, and they can get the fake accounts removed.

Key Takeaways

  • You cannot win a lottery you never entered — if you did not enter, the message is fake by definition.
  • A real prize is free — no processing fee, tax, delivery charge, or customs duty. Anyone who asks you to pay to receive a prize is a scammer.
  • Never share your CNIC copy, bank details, password, PIN, or OTP to claim a prize — a real organiser never asks for these.
  • Never click a link in a prize message — check the prize on the organisation's real website, typed into your browser yourself.
  • Urgency is a weapon — "claim in 2 hours or lose it" is designed to stop you from checking. Slow down and verify.
  • Verify in five minutes — search the message text and the phone number, and call the organisation on its official number from its real website.
  • A screenshot of a winner list or a certificate proves nothing — both are easy to fake.
  • If you already paid or shared details, act fast — call your bank or wallet, change your passwords, keep the evidence, and report to the FIA Cybercrime Wing.
  • Warn others — one message in your family group can save the next person from the same scam.
