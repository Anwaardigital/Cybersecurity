How to Secure Your Instagram Account: Stop Hackers, Scams and Fake Followers (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 22 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 12 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

Instagram is where millions of people in Pakistan share their photos, run small businesses, and follow creators — and that is exactly why criminals target it. A stolen Instagram account is valuable to a thief: with your account they can message your followers pretending to be you and ask for emergency money, post crypto "giveaway" scams to your audience, take over your business page, sell your account to someone else, and read years of your private messages and photos. And Instagram scams are getting more convincing every year: fake "your account will be deleted" warnings, fake blue-tick verification offers, fake copyright-strike messages, and cloned accounts that copy your photo and name. The good news is that Instagram has strong built-in protections — two-factor authentication, login activity checks, privacy controls, and hidden-words filters — but most people never turn them on. This guide explains how Instagram accounts actually get stolen, the warning signs, and the exact settings to lock down today, plus what to do if your account is already hacked. Everything here is defensive: protecting your own account, your own family, and your own business.

How Instagram accounts get stolen

Criminals rarely break into Instagram's servers. Instead, they trick you, or trick the systems around your account. These are the five methods used most often:

  • The fake login page (phishing — the most common). You get a direct message, email, or notification: "Your account violated our rules", "Copyright violation — verify now", "You have been selected for verification", or "Your account will be deleted in 24 hours". The link opens a page that looks exactly like Instagram's login screen. You type your username and password, and the thief captures them. The real Instagram login page is only at instagram.com or in the official Instagram app — never a link someone sent you. Our fake copyright-strike guide explains one common version of this trick in detail.
  • Cloned accounts and impersonation. The thief copies your profile photo, your name, and your bio, and creates a new account that looks like you. Then they follow your followers and message them asking for money, a mobile top-up, or an "investment" in a crypto scheme. Your real followers cannot tell the fake account from the real one at a glance. This is one of the most reported Instagram scams in Pakistan.
  • Password reuse. If you use the same password on Instagram that you used on another website, and that website leaks its passwords in a data breach, the thief simply tries the same password on Instagram. It works far more often than people think. Our password security guide explains why every account needs its own password, and our password manager guide shows how to manage them safely.
  • Fake "free followers", "who viewed your profile" and "verification" apps. "Get 10,000 free followers", "See who viewed your profile", "Get the blue tick for free" — these apps and websites ask you to log in with Instagram or give them your password. Many of them harvest your account and post spam, follow hundreds of accounts, and message your followers with scams. Instagram does not show who viewed your profile, and no third-party app can give you free followers or a blue tick.
  • SIM swapping and stolen login codes. If your Instagram login code goes to your phone number by SMS, a criminal who takes over your SIM can receive your codes. Our SIM swapping protection guide explains how to stop this.

7 warning signs your Instagram account is compromised

If you notice any of these, check your account immediately using the steps below:

  • Your password suddenly stops working and Instagram says it is incorrect, even though you typed it correctly. This is the clearest sign someone changed it.
  • Your email address or phone number on the account changed without you changing it, or you get an email saying your password or email was changed that you did not request.
  • Posts, stories, or messages appear that you never made — especially messages asking your followers for money or sharing crypto "investment" links.
  • Your followers tell you they got a follow or a message from a second account with your photo and name. That is a cloned account — report it (Step 6).
  • You see an unknown device or location in your login activity — a phone, computer, or city you do not recognise.
  • Your profile photo, name, bio, or website link changed without you changing it, or you suddenly follow hundreds of accounts you never followed.
  • Login codes arrive when you did not try to log in. Someone just tried to log in as you. Do not ignore these — turn on two-factor authentication right now.

If several of these apply, treat your account as stolen and jump to the recovery section at the end of this guide. Our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the wider warning signs that apply to your whole phone as well.

Step 1: Turn on two-factor authentication (the single most important step)

Two-factor authentication (2FA) adds a second check after your password — a code from an authenticator app or a text message. Even if a thief steals your password, they cannot log in without that code. Turn it on now; it takes less than two minutes:

  1. Open Instagram, tap your profile picture (bottom right), then tap the menu (three lines, top right), then Accounts Centre.
  2. Tap Password and security, then Two-factor authentication, and choose your account.
  3. Pick Authentication app (the safest choice — Google Authenticator, Microsoft Authenticator, or any similar app) or Text message (SMS). An authenticator app is safer than SMS because it cannot be stolen by SIM swapping.
  4. Follow the steps to link the app or confirm your phone number, then save the backup codes Instagram shows you. Keep these codes somewhere safe — on paper, or in a password manager — because they are your way back in if you lose your phone.

After this, every new login needs your password and a code from your phone. A thief with only your password is stopped. Our two-factor authentication guide explains the same idea for your Google, WhatsApp and Facebook accounts.

Step 2: Use a strong, unique password — and change it if it ever leaked

Your Instagram password should exist nowhere else. If you used the same password on another site that leaked, change your Instagram password today:

  • Make it long, not clever. A password of 16 or more characters — three or four random words joined together, for example — is far stronger than a short password with symbols. Avoid your name, your birthday, your children's names, or 123456.
  • Never reuse it. One password per account. A password manager remembers them all so you only remember one. Our password manager guide shows how to set one up.
  • Change it after any breach. If you hear that a website you use was hacked, change your Instagram password even if Instagram was not the site that leaked. Our data breach recovery guide explains what to do after a leak.
  • Never share your password with anyone — not a friend, not an "Instagram agent", not a shop that offers to "grow" your followers or get you verified. Instagram staff will never ask for your password.

Step 3: Check where you are logged in — and log out strangers

Instagram lets you see every phone, tablet and computer logged into your account. A thief who logs in on their own device can read your messages without you knowing. Checking takes one minute:

  1. Open Instagram, go to your profile, tap the menu, then Accounts Centre > Password and security.
  2. Tap Where you're logged in. You will see every device, with its location and last active time.
  3. Look at every entry. You should recognise each one — your own phone, your own laptop. If you see a device, browser, or city you do not recognise, tap it and choose Log out immediately.
  4. If you find a session you do not recognise, also change your password and turn on two-factor authentication (Steps 1 and 2), because someone had access to your account.
  5. Turn on login alerts in the same menu, so Instagram tells you by email or notification every time a new device logs in.

Make this a monthly habit — the same way you check your bank balance. A device you did not log in from is proof your account was accessed, even if nothing else looks wrong.

Step 4: Lock down your privacy settings

Your privacy settings decide what strangers can see and do to you on Instagram. Go to Settings > Privacy and set these:

  • Private account: on (for personal accounts). A private account means only people you approve can see your posts, stories and reels. This stops scammers building a cloned account from your photos and details. Business and creator accounts cannot be private, so if you run a business account, use the other settings below instead.
  • Who can message you: limit message requests. In Settings > Privacy > Messages, set message requests from people you do not follow to "Don't receive requests". This cuts most scam messages before they reach your inbox.
  • Who can tag and mention you: "People you follow" only. This stops a scammer tagging you in a scam post that then appears to your followers as if you shared it. Turn on "Manually approve tags" too, so nothing appears on your profile without your permission.
  • Hidden words: on. In Settings > Privacy > Hidden words, turn on "Hide comments" and "Hide message requests" for offensive words. This filters most spam and scam comments automatically.
  • Who can see your activity status and story sharing: off. Turn off "Show activity status" and stop other people resharing your stories. Your location and your online times tell a thief when you are away from home.
  • Do not link your phone number publicly. Your phone number is the key to your account. Never put it in your bio, and in Accounts Centre > Personal details keep it visible only to you.
  • Block and report, don't argue. If a stranger messages you with a prize, a job offer, a crypto investment, or a threat, do not reply. Open their profile, tap the three dots, choose Block, then Report. Reporting helps Instagram ban the account.

Step 5: Spot the scams that target Instagram users

Most Instagram scams follow a small number of scripts. Once you know them, they are easy to spot:

  • Cloned accounts asking for money. A copy of your friend's (or your own) account messages you: "I am in trouble, send me money urgently" or "Send me a JazzCash/Easypaisa transfer". Before sending any money, call the person on their real number. A voice or video call takes thirty seconds and stops this scam completely.
  • Fake verification and blue-tick offers. A message claims Instagram wants to verify you, or offers you a blue tick for a fee. Real verification is free and is requested inside the Instagram app itself, in Settings > Accounts Centre > Meta Verified or the verification request form. Nobody on Instagram can sell you a blue tick.
  • "Your account will be deleted" warnings. A message or email claims your account violated a policy and will be deleted in 24 hours unless you click a link and "verify". Instagram does not delete accounts this way, and it does not send warnings through random accounts or direct messages. Delete the message.
  • Fake copyright strikes. "Your reel violated copyright, appeal here" — with a link to a fake login page. Our fake copyright-strike guide explains this trick in full.
  • Crypto and "double your money" giveaways. A hacked celebrity or creator account posts: "Send 0.1 Bitcoin and get 0.2 back" or "I am giving away money to my followers". Real creators never ask you to send money to receive money. Any message asking you to send money to receive money is a scam.
  • Fake brand deals and "influencer" offers. A message offers you a paid brand deal or free products, then asks for a "registration fee", your bank details, or your login. Real brands contact you by email, never ask for a fee, and never ask for your password. Our brand deal scams guide explains this trick in detail.
  • Fake job offers and "online earning" messages. A message offers easy work from home, then asks for a "registration fee" or your bank details. Real employers do not hire over Instagram direct messages and never ask for a fee. Our fake job offers guide explains the job-scam version of this trick in detail.

The rule for all of these is the same: slow down. Scammers create urgency — "now", "today only", "limited offer" — because a person in a hurry does not think. If a message makes you feel rushed or frightened, stop, and check it through a different channel: call the person, visit the official website yourself, or ask a family member.

Step 6: Protect your followers from your cloned account

You cannot stop a thief copying your public photo — but you can make it much harder, and you can make sure your followers spot the fake:

  • Make personal accounts private. A private account hides your photos and reels from strangers, so a thief has almost nothing to copy. If you run a business account, keep your personal photos off it.
  • Watermark your original content. Adding your username to your photos and reels makes a cloned account easier to spot — the thief's copy still carries your name.
  • Tell your followers how to spot the clone. A real message from you will come from your old account, not a brand-new one with few followers and few posts. Ask your family to check: if the account was created recently, has almost no followers, and only your photo — it is fake.
  • Report a clone immediately. If you find a fake copy of your account, open it, tap the three dots, choose Report, then Report account, then It's pretending to be someone else, then Me. Ask your followers to report it too — several reports get it removed faster. Do the same for a cloned account of a friend, choosing A friend instead.

Step 7: Keep your phone and the Instagram app safe

Your Instagram account is only as safe as the phone it lives on:

  • Only install the official Instagram app from the Google Play Store or Apple App Store. Never install an "Instagram mod", "Instagram++", "GB Instagram", or any app that promises extra Instagram features — many of them steal your login session. Our fake apps guide explains how lookalike apps steal your account.
  • Keep Instagram updated. Updates fix security holes. Turn on automatic updates for Instagram in your app store, or check for updates every week.
  • Lock your phone. A thief with your unlocked phone can open Instagram and change your password, your email, and your recovery options. Use a PIN, fingerprint, or face lock.
  • Log out on shared devices. If you ever log into Instagram on a friend's phone or a shop's computer, log out before you leave — and never tick "Remember me" or "Keep me logged in" on a device that is not yours. Our public computer safety guide explains this in detail.
  • Check app permissions. In your phone settings, look at what Instagram can access. It needs storage and camera to work. If an app calling itself Instagram asks for your SMS messages or your call logs, it is fake — uninstall it immediately.
  • Review third-party apps connected to Instagram. In Settings > Privacy > Apps and websites (on the Instagram website), remove any app or website you do not recognise. Old connected apps keep access to your account until you remove them.

Your account was hacked — recover it now

If you are locked out of Instagram, act quickly — the thief is messaging your followers right now. Do these steps in order:

  1. Use Instagram's own recovery. On the Instagram login screen, tap Forgot password? and enter your username, email, or phone number. Instagram will send you a login link or code. If the thief changed your email, tap Need more help? and follow the identity check — Instagram may ask you to record a short video of yourself to confirm you are the real owner.
  2. Check your email for Instagram's security message. If your email was changed, Instagram sends a message to your old email saying "your email was changed" with a "secure my account" link. Tap it immediately — it reverses the change.
  3. Change your password immediately once you are back in. Pick a new, strong, unique password (Step 2).
  4. Log out all other sessions. Go to Accounts Centre > Password and security > Where you're logged in and log out every device you do not recognise. This logs the thief out of your account on every device except yours.
  5. Turn on two-factor authentication if you did not have it on (Step 1). Also check that your email address and phone number on the account are still yours — if the thief changed them, add your real ones back.
  6. Warn your followers immediately. As soon as you are back in, post a story and a post: "My Instagram was hacked. Do not send money or click links from my account before today." The thief's most common trick is asking your followers for emergency money in your name.
  7. Report the crime. In Pakistan, Instagram hacking and the fraud that follows it can be reported to the FIA Cybercrime Wing. Keep screenshots of the scam messages, the thief's account link, and any money-transfer details. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.

Key Takeaways

  • Turn on two-factor authentication today — a password plus a code from your phone stops most Instagram theft. Use an authenticator app if you can.
  • Use a strong, unique password that you use nowhere else, and change it after any data breach.
  • Check "Where you're logged in" every month and log out any device you do not recognise.
  • Make personal accounts private, limit who can message you, and turn on hidden words.
  • Never click a "your account will be deleted" link — Instagram does not warn you that way, and the link leads to a fake login page.
  • Never pay for a blue tick or "free followers" — verification is free inside the app, and follower apps steal your account.
  • Before sending money to anyone on Instagram, call them on their real number first — a thirty-second call stops the cloned-account scam.
  • If your account is hacked, use "Forgot password?", check your old email for the "secure my account" link, change your password, log out all sessions, and warn your followers — then report it to Instagram and the FIA.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your own account, your own family, and your own business from scammers.
