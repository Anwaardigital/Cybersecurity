How to Spot a Fake Website Before You Enter Your Details (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 7 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 8 min read
Ethical Use Only: This guide is for defensive protection only — how to protect yourself from fake websites. Never create fake websites, copy another site's design to deceive people, or use these techniques to trick anyone. Everything here is for defensive security education.

A fake website looks exactly like the real thing: the same logo, the same colours, the same layout. The difference is invisible until it is too late — you type in your bank login, your email password or your card number, and it goes straight to a criminal. Fake shops, fake bank pages, fake delivery tracking pages and fake giveaway pages appear every day, usually through a text message, a social media ad or a search result. The good news is that every fake website leaves clues. This guide teaches you seven quick checks that take about sixty seconds — and what to do if you already typed your details into a site you now distrust.

What a fake website is

A fake website is a copy of a real website built to steal from you. Some pretend to be online shops selling popular products at impossible prices; others copy a bank, an email provider or a delivery company and ask you to "log in" or "confirm your details". The page itself is harmless to look at — the danger starts the moment you type something into it or pay through it. Fake sites spread through text messages about "missed deliveries", social media ads for "closing-down sales", and even paid ads at the top of search results. They work because they look professional: criminals copy the real site's design pixel for pixel, so "it looks real" is never a good test. The checks below are.

Check 1: Read the web address slowly

The web address is the one thing a fake site cannot copy exactly, so read it character by character. Look for small misspellings and letter swaps, such as a zero instead of the letter O, or an extra letter in a brand name. Watch for extra words attached to a real brand — "amazon-deals" or "paypal-secure-login" are not the real sites. Be suspicious of strange endings, such as a shop that ends in .xyz, .top or .buzz instead of a normal ending. And read the address up to the first single slash: everything before that slash is the real address, so in "login.amazon.com.example-shop.net", the real site is example-shop.net, not Amazon. If the address does not match the brand exactly, close the tab.

Check 2: A padlock does not mean a site is safe

Many people believe the little padlock icon in the address bar means a website is trustworthy. It does not. The padlock only means your connection to the site is encrypted — it says nothing about who runs the site. Criminals put padlocks on their fake sites too, because the certificate that creates the padlock is free and takes minutes to get. A site without a padlock is certainly unsafe, but a site with one is only "encrypted", not "honest". Never let the padlock be the reason you trust a site.

Check 3: Look for a real business behind the site

A genuine business wants you to find it. Look for an About page that names real people or a real company, a full postal address, and a phone number or an email address on the same domain as the website. Then test it: does the phone number work, does anyone reply to the email? A shop with no address, no phone number, and only a web form for contact is hiding something. Also look at the site's age in a general sense: a shop that claims to be a famous brand but has no reviews anywhere, no social media presence, and no history is almost certainly a copy that appeared days ago. Real businesses leave footprints.

Check 4: Prices and promises that are too good

If a brand-new phone is selling for a tenth of its price, or a "closing down sale" offers 90% off everything, stop and think. Criminals use impossible prices because greed switches off caution — you hurry to buy before the deal disappears, and you skip the checks. The same trick appears as prizes: "You have won a gift card, pay a small delivery fee to claim it" is never a real prize. A genuine discount is believable; a price that makes no business sense is the bait. When a deal feels too good to be true, it is.

Check 5: How do they want you to pay?

The payment method is one of the strongest clues. A normal shop accepts cards through a proper checkout, which gives you a chance to dispute the payment with your bank. A fake site pushes you towards payments you cannot reverse: gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers, or a payment sent to a personal account. If a "shop" asks you to pay with gift cards, or tells you to send money to a person's name rather than a company, close the page immediately. No genuine business takes payment that way.

Check 6: Search the name before you pay

Before you pay or log in, open a new tab and search for the shop or website name together with the word "scam" or "reviews". Real complaints appear quickly, and other victims often name the exact fake address. Just as telling is finding nothing at all: a shop that claims thousands of happy customers but has zero reviews, zero mentions and zero history anywhere is not a popular shop — it is a new fake. This check takes thirty seconds and catches most fake sites before any money moves.

Check 7: Sloppy words and stolen photos

Read the page like a proofreader. Fake sites are often built in a hurry, so look for spelling mistakes, strange grammar, blurry logos and buttons that lead nowhere. Product photos are often stolen from real shops — if the same photo appears on five different "shops" with five different names, none of them is real. Prices in the wrong currency, a checkout page that switches language halfway through, and pop-ups that beg you to "act now" are all signs of a rushed copy. A real business polishes its pages; a fake one copies them.

What to do if you already typed your details in

Do not panic — act fast instead. If you entered a password, change it immediately, and change it everywhere else you used the same password. Turn on two-factor authentication on that account so the stolen password alone is not enough. If you entered card details or paid, call your bank straight away and tell them what happened; a card payment can often be disputed, but gift cards and cryptocurrency almost never come back. Check your bank and email statements for the next few weeks for anything you do not recognise. Then report the site so it can be taken down: report it through your browser's "Report phishing" option, and report the scam to your local cybercrime authority — our guide on how to report cybercrime and online scams walks through the steps. Finally, warn the people who sent you the link: they may have been tricked too.

Your 60-second checklist before paying or logging in

  • Read the web address slowly — does it match the brand exactly, with no extra words or misspellings?
  • Ignore the padlock — it only means the connection is encrypted, not that the site is honest.
  • Find a real business: an About page, a real address, a working phone number.
  • Ask yourself if the price or prize is believable — too good to be true means it is a trap.
  • Check the payment method — gift cards, crypto or a personal account means stop.
  • Search the site name with the word "scam" before you pay anything.
  • If anything feels wrong, close the tab. A real shop will still be there tomorrow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a fake site harm my phone just by visiting it?
Visiting alone rarely harms an updated phone. The real danger is typing in your details, or downloading a file the site offers. Keep your browser and phone updated, and never download "security" software a website tells you to install.

Is HTTPS enough to trust a site?
No. HTTPS only encrypts the connection. Fake sites use HTTPS too, so always check the address and the business behind the site as well.

What if the site looks exactly like the real one?
That is normal — criminals copy the design. The address is the check that matters: one wrong letter means it is not the real site, however perfect it looks.

I paid a fake shop. Can I get my money back?
Call your bank immediately. Card payments can often be disputed, and the sooner you report it, the better the chance. Payments by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency are almost never recoverable, which is why fake sites prefer them.

Should I report a fake website?
Yes. Reporting gets the site taken down and protects the next person. Use your browser's phishing report option and your local cybercrime reporting channel.

Key Takeaways

  • Fake websites copy real designs perfectly — "it looks real" is never a good test.
  • The web address is the check that matters: read it slowly, up to the first slash.
  • A padlock means encrypted, not honest — fake sites have padlocks too.
  • No contact details, impossible prices and unreversible payments are the three biggest red flags.
  • Search the site name with "scam" before you pay — thirty seconds can save your money.
  • If you already entered details, change passwords, turn on two-factor authentication, call your bank, and report the site.
