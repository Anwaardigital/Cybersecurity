How to Spot and Avoid Fake Charity and Donation Scams (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 27 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 12 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

You see a photo of a sick child in a hospital bed, or a video of flood victims standing in waist-deep water, and a message underneath: "Please donate — even Rs. 100 can save a life. JazzCash: 03XX-XXXXXXX." Your heart says yes before your brain has had time to ask a single question. That is exactly what charity scammers count on. A fake charity scam is one of the cruellest online frauds, because it steals twice: it takes your money, and it takes the help that a real person in need would have received. In Pakistan, where giving — sadqa, zakat, and donations for hospitals, mosques, orphanages, and disaster relief — is a deep part of everyday life, these scams spread fast on WhatsApp, Facebook, and SMS, especially during Ramadan, after floods and earthquakes, and whenever a heartbreaking story goes viral. This guide explains how fake charity scams actually work, the ten red flags that give them away, how to verify any charity or fundraiser in a few minutes before you send a single rupee, how to donate safely, and what to do if you have already sent money to a scammer. Everything here is defensive: protecting your money, your personal information, and the real charities that deserve your generosity.

Why charity scams work so well

Most online scams work by creating fear — a fake bank warning, a prize you might lose, a locked account. Charity scams work the opposite way: they create kindness, urgency, and guilt. Understanding the psychology is your first line of defence:

  • Emotion comes before thinking. A photo of a suffering child or an injured accident victim triggers an instant emotional response. Scammers know that if you feel strongly enough, you will donate before you verify. Real charities also use emotional photos — that is what makes this scam hard to spot — but a real charity can prove who it is when you ask.
  • Urgency shuts down checking. "The operation is tomorrow morning." "The family will be evicted tonight." "Flood water is rising right now." When a message says someone will suffer unless you pay immediately, there is no time to check the facts — and that is the point. A genuine charity will still be there tomorrow, and it will never punish you for taking ten minutes to verify it.
  • Small amounts feel safe. Scammers often ask for Rs. 100, Rs. 200, or Rs. 500. The amount is small enough that you do not bother checking, and small enough that you may never report it. But when ten thousand people each send Rs. 200, the scammer has collected two million rupees.
  • Trust travels through people you know. Most fake charity appeals do not come from a stranger — they arrive forwarded from a family member, a friend, or a respected person in your mosque or community group. You trust the sender, so you trust the message. But the person who forwarded it probably never checked it either. Our SMS scams guide explains how scammers use forwarded messages and fake urgency on every channel, not just SMS.
  • Giving feels good, and questioning feels rude. Many people hesitate to ask for proof because it feels like doubting someone's suffering. Scammers rely on that politeness. Remember: a real charity is never offended when you ask for its registration number or its official bank account. Only a scammer gets angry when you ask questions.

How fake charity scams reach you

Charity scammers use every channel where people share emotional stories. Here are the most common ways they appear in Pakistan:

  • WhatsApp forwards and groups. The most common route. A long emotional message, often with photos of a sick child or a hospital bill, ends with a personal JazzCash or Easypaisa number and a plea to forward it to everyone you know. The message is usually copied and pasted, so the same appeal — with the same photos and the same number — circulates for months or years, long after the original situation (if it was ever real) has changed. Our WhatsApp security guide explains how hijacked accounts spread these messages.
  • Facebook posts, pages, and groups. A new Facebook page with a touching name — "Help Ahmed Fight Cancer" or "Flood Relief Pakistan" — posts stolen photos and a donation link. The page may have been created days ago, have very few real followers, and use a personal bank account or mobile wallet for donations. Facebook fundraisers can be legitimate, but a page with no history, no website, and no registered organisation behind it deserves a careful check.
  • Fake charity websites. A professional-looking website with the name and logo of a well-known charity — sometimes almost identical to the real one, with one letter changed in the web address. You find it through a Google search or a link in a message, and the "Donate" button takes you to a payment page controlled by the scammer. Our guide to spotting fake websites shows how to check a web address before you trust it.
  • SMS and phone calls. A text message claims to be from a hospital, an orphanage, or a disaster-relief fund, with a short link and a mobile wallet number. A caller may claim to be collecting for a mosque, a madrassa, or a flood victim, and pressure you to send money immediately. No legitimate charity collects donations by cold-calling strangers and asking for a mobile wallet transfer.
  • Disaster-relief scams. Within hours of a flood, earthquake, or bombing, fake relief campaigns appear. They use real news photos and videos from the disaster — stolen from news channels and social media — and collect "emergency donations" that never reach any victim. This is the fastest-growing form of charity fraud, because the news itself creates the urgency.
  • Fake crowdfunding pages. A fundraising page for a medical emergency, with a heartbreaking story and a target amount. Some crowdfunding is genuine, but scammers create pages using stolen medical documents and photos of other people's children. When the page has no hospital name, no verifiable patient details, and donations go to a personal account, be very careful.
  • Religious appeals. Appeals for zakat, sadqa, mosque construction, or Quran distribution are especially common during Ramadan and on religious occasions. Scammers exploit the trust people place in religious giving. Remember: zakat has rules about who can receive it, and a genuine religious organisation can always show its registration and its accounts.
  • Fake celebrity or influencer endorsements. A video or post appears to show a famous person asking for donations to a cause. Often it is a deepfake video or an old clip reused with a new donation number. Our deepfake scams guide explains how to check whether a video is real.

The 10 red flags of a fake charity

Check any donation appeal against this list before you send money. One red flag is a reason to pause; two or three mean you should not donate until you have verified everything:

  1. The donation goes to a personal mobile wallet or personal bank account. A registered charity collects donations in the organisation's own bank account, in the organisation's name. If the JazzCash, Easypaisa, or bank account is in a person's name — "Send to Muhammad Ali, 03XX-XXXXXXX" — that is the single biggest warning sign. Real charities do not collect public donations through a volunteer's personal wallet.
  2. There is no registration number or verifiable organisation. A genuine charity in Pakistan is registered — for example with the Securities and Exchange Commission of Pakistan (SECP), a provincial charity commission, or as a registered trust or society — and it can tell you its registration number. If the appeal names no organisation at all, or the organisation has no registration, no office address, and no website, treat it as unverified.
  3. Extreme urgency and pressure. "Send now or the child dies tonight." "Only 2 hours left." "If you do not donate, you are responsible." Real charities explain a need; scammers manufacture a deadline. Pressure is a sales tactic, not a sign of a genuine emergency.
  4. Vague details that cannot be checked. "A poor family in Lahore needs help." "A child in a hospital needs an operation." No full name, no hospital name, no city, no doctor, no case number — nothing you could actually verify. Real medical appeals name the hospital and the treating doctor, because those details can be checked.
  5. Photos that appear everywhere. The same child's photo used in five different appeals, for five different diseases, in five different cities. You can check this yourself: save the photo and do a reverse image search (Google Images or a similar tool) to see where else it has appeared. If the same photo has been used for different causes, it is stolen.
  6. The web address or page looks slightly wrong. A well-known charity's name with a spelling mistake, an extra word, or a strange domain ending — for example, a ".xyz" or ".top" address instead of the charity's real ".org.pk" website. Always type the charity's web address yourself, or use a link from its verified social media page, instead of clicking a link in a message.
  7. The appeal asks for unusual payment methods. Gift cards, cryptocurrency, wire transfers to a foreign account, or a payment through an unfamiliar app. Legitimate Pakistani charities accept bank transfers, cheques, and well-known local payment methods in the organisation's name. Our crypto scams guide explains why cryptocurrency payments are a favourite of scammers: they are very hard to reverse.
  8. You are asked for personal information. A donation form that asks for your CNIC number, your bank account details, your OTP, or your debit card PIN is not a charity — it is identity theft. No charity ever needs your OTP or your card PIN. Our identity theft protection guide explains what criminals do with stolen personal information.
  9. The story keeps changing or the money never ends. The same appeal returns every few months with a new deadline, a new amount, and the same photos. Or the fundraiser reaches its target and immediately starts a new one for the same person, with no update about what the first collection achieved. Real charities publish updates and accounts.
  10. You cannot contact a real person at a real office. No landline number, no physical office address, no email on the organisation's own domain — only a mobile number and a WhatsApp account. Call the number and ask questions: a genuine organisation will answer patiently; a scammer will rush you, get angry, or stop replying.

How to verify a charity in 5 minutes

Before you donate to any charity, fundraiser, or appeal you have not used before, spend five minutes on these checks. It is the difference between helping someone and funding a scammer:

  • Search the name plus the word "scam" or "fraud". Type the charity's name or the person's name into Google, followed by "scam", "fake", or "fraud". Also search the mobile number or bank account number from the appeal. If other people have been scammed, someone has usually posted a warning. Also search the name on Facebook and X to see what people are saying.
  • Check the registration. Ask for the registration number and the name of the authority it is registered with. In Pakistan, many genuine charities are registered with SECP as a not-for-profit company, or with a provincial charity commission, or as a registered trust. You can check an SECP-registered organisation on SECP's website. If the organisation cannot give you a registration number, do not donate.
  • Verify the bank account name. Before sending money, check that the account holder's name matches the charity's name — not a person's name. Most banks and mobile wallets show the account holder's name before you confirm a transfer. If the name does not match, stop.
  • Visit the official website yourself. Do not click the link in the message. Search for the charity's name and open its official website, or open its verified Facebook page, and use the donation details listed there. Compare the bank account and wallet numbers with the ones in the message. If they are different, the message is fake — even if everything else looks right.
  • Call the charity directly. Find the charity's official phone number from its own website — not from the appeal message — and call it. Ask whether the fundraiser is genuine and whether the bank account in the message belongs to them. A two-minute call can save your donation.
  • For medical appeals, call the hospital. If the appeal names a hospital and a patient, call the hospital's main number and ask whether the patient is admitted there. Hospitals will usually confirm whether a named patient exists, and many have their own official donation desks for patients who cannot pay. Never send money to a personal account for a hospital bill without confirming the case with the hospital first.
  • Check the photos with a reverse image search. Save the photo from the appeal and search for it on Google Images or a similar reverse-search tool. If the same photo appears on other websites for other causes, or on a stock-photo site, it has been stolen.
  • Look at the page's history. On Facebook, open the page's "About" section and check when it was created and whether it has changed its name. A page created last week, with only donation posts and no real history, is suspicious. A genuine charity page has months or years of posts, photos of real events, and comments from real people.
  • Ask for a receipt and a record. A real charity gives you a proper receipt with its name, registration number, and the amount. If the person collecting cannot give you any receipt, your donation is untraceable — which is exactly what a scammer wants.

Safe donation habits — give generously, give wisely

Giving is a good thing, and this guide is not telling you to stop. It is telling you how to make sure your generosity actually reaches the people you want to help:

  • Choose your charities in advance. Pick two or three well-known, registered charities you trust — for example, established hospitals, orphanages, or relief organisations with a long public record — and give to them regularly. When a new appeal arrives, you can simply say, "I already give to my chosen charities," instead of deciding under pressure.
  • Donate through the charity's own website or office. Use the official website, the charity's own app, or visit its office in person. Never donate through a link forwarded in a chat, and never send money to a personal mobile wallet number from an appeal.
  • Pay by bank transfer to the organisation's account. A bank transfer to an account in the charity's name leaves a record. Cash handed to a stranger, or a wallet transfer to a personal number, leaves almost no trail. Keep your receipt and your transfer confirmation.
  • Never share your OTP, PIN, or card details. No charity, bank, or government department will ever ask for your OTP or your ATM PIN — not on a call, not in a message, not on a form. Anyone who asks is a scammer. Our fake prize scams guide explains the same rule, because scammers reuse it in every kind of fraud.
  • Do not let anyone rush you. "Donate now" is not an emergency. A genuine need will still be there after you have spent ten minutes verifying it. If someone pressures you, insults you, or says you will be punished for not giving, walk away — that is manipulation, not charity.
  • Be extra careful in Ramadan and after disasters. These are the two peak seasons for charity fraud. Scammers prepare fake campaigns in advance for Ramadan, and launch fake relief appeals within hours of a flood or earthquake. Double your checks at these times.
  • Check before you forward. Forwarding an unverified appeal makes you part of the scam, even with good intentions. Before you forward a donation message, do the five-minute checks above. If you cannot verify it, do not forward it — or forward it with a clear warning that it is unverified. One careful person in a family group can protect dozens of people.
  • Keep a record of your donations. Note the charity's name, the amount, the date, and the receipt number. This helps you at tax time, helps you spot duplicate or fake appeals, and gives you evidence if you ever need to report a fraud.

Zakat and religious giving — extra care

Zakat is an obligation, not a choice, and scammers know that people feel a duty to pay it quickly, especially in Ramadan. A few rules keep your zakat safe:

  • Give zakat only to verified recipients. Zakat has specific rules about who may receive it. A genuine zakat-collecting organisation can explain its zakat policy, show its registration, and tell you how it distributes zakat separately from other donations. If an appeal cannot explain where your zakat goes, do not send it.
  • Prefer organisations you know. Established zakat institutions with published accounts and years of public work are the safest choice. A new organisation that appears only in Ramadan, collects through personal wallet numbers, and disappears afterwards is a warning sign.
  • Mosque and madrassa collections. If someone collects in the name of a mosque or madrassa, check with the mosque's own committee or imam before giving. Many genuine mosque collections are organised locally and honestly — but scammers also use mosque names because people trust them without checking.
  • Ask for a zakat receipt. A genuine organisation issues a receipt that says the amount was received as zakat. This is your record, and it is also proof that the organisation treats zakat properly.

What to do if you already donated to a fake charity

If you sent money and now suspect it was a scam, act quickly — the sooner you act, the better your chances:

  • Contact your bank or wallet provider immediately. Call your bank, JazzCash, or Easypaisa helpline and explain that you sent money to a suspected fraud. Ask whether the transaction can be reversed or the recipient's account can be frozen. Reversals are not guaranteed, especially for wallet transfers, but they are sometimes possible if you report within hours. Do not wait until tomorrow.
  • Change your passwords if you shared any details. If you filled in a donation form with your email, phone number, or any account information, change the passwords of those accounts — starting with your email and your bank — and turn on two-factor authentication. If you shared your CNIC number, watch your accounts closely and read our data breach recovery guide. If you shared an OTP or a PIN, call your bank immediately and block your card.
  • Save all the evidence. Take screenshots of the appeal message, the page, the website, the payment confirmation, and the recipient's number or account details. Save the chat history. Do not delete anything — this evidence is what investigators need, and the scammer will delete their page as soon as people start reporting it.
  • Report it. Report the scam to the FIA Cybercrime Wing through its online complaint system, and to the PTA if it came by SMS or phone call. Report the page or account to Facebook, WhatsApp, or whichever platform carried it. Also report it to your bank's fraud department. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and links.
  • Warn the people who forwarded it to you. Tell the family member or friend who shared the appeal that it was fake, and ask them to tell the group. This stops the scam from spreading further, and it protects the next person who would have donated.
  • Do not pay a "recovery" fee. After a charity scam, victims are sometimes contacted by a second scammer offering to recover the money for a fee. That is the same fraud wearing a different mask. No legitimate recovery service asks for an upfront fee on WhatsApp. Report the recovery offer as well.
  • Do not feel ashamed. Charity scammers succeed because they exploit kindness, not because their victims are foolish. Millions of good, careful people have been caught by these scams. Reporting it helps everyone — and staying silent only protects the scammer.

How to help real charities instead

The best answer to fake charities is to support real ones well:

  • Give regularly, not only in a crisis. A monthly donation to a trusted charity, even a small one, helps it plan and helps real people far more than a panicked one-time payment to a stranger's wallet number.
  • Visit when you can. If a charity works in your city, visit its office, hospital, school, or orphanage. Seeing the work with your own eyes is the strongest verification there is — and it builds a relationship you can trust.
  • Volunteer your time. If you are unsure about money, give your time instead. Genuine charities always need volunteers, and volunteering shows you exactly how an organisation works.
  • Share verified appeals. When you find a genuine appeal — one you have checked yourself — share it. Your verification protects the people who trust your judgement.

Key Takeaways

  • Fake charity scams steal your money and the help real people need — they work by triggering emotion and urgency, especially on WhatsApp and Facebook, during Ramadan and after disasters.
  • Never donate to a personal JazzCash, Easypaisa, or bank account from an appeal. A real charity collects in the organisation's own account, in the organisation's name.
  • Check the 10 red flags before you donate: no registration, extreme urgency, vague details, stolen photos, a slightly wrong website, unusual payment methods, requests for your OTP or PIN, and no way to contact a real office.
  • Verify in five minutes: search the name plus "scam", check the registration, confirm the bank account name matches the charity, visit the official website yourself, and call the charity on its own published number.
  • For medical appeals, call the hospital directly — never send money to a personal account for a hospital bill without confirming the case first.
  • Never share your OTP, PIN, or card details with anyone, for any donation. No real charity ever asks for them.
  • Check before you forward — forwarding an unverified appeal spreads the scam to everyone who trusts you.
  • If you already paid a scammer: call your bank or wallet provider immediately, change any passwords you shared, save all the evidence, and report it to the FIA Cybercrime Wing and the PTA.
  • Give generously — to charities you have verified. Choose trusted organisations in advance, donate through their official channels, and keep your receipts.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your money, your information, and the real charities that deserve your generosity.
