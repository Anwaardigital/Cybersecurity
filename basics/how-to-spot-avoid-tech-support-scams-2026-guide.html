How to Spot and Avoid Tech Support Scams (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 25 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 13 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

You are browsing a website when your whole screen suddenly turns red. A loud alarm starts beeping. A big warning says: "WARNING! Your computer is infected with a virus! Your files, photos and bank details are at risk! Call Microsoft Support immediately at this number — do not turn off your computer!" Or your phone rings, and a polite caller says: "Hello sir, I am calling from Microsoft technical support. Your computer has sent us an error message. Hackers are attacking your system right now. Please let me help you fix it." In both cases, your heart beats faster. That fear is exactly what the scammer is counting on. A tech support scam is a trick where a criminal pretends to be from a famous company — usually Microsoft, Google, Apple, or your antivirus company — and convinces you that your computer or phone is in danger. Then they ask you to pay for a "fix", to give them remote access to your computer, or to hand over your bank details. There is no virus. There is no error message. Your computer never sent anything to Microsoft. The "support agent" is a criminal, often working from a fake call centre, and their only goal is your money and your information. Victims in Pakistan lose money in many ways: a "repair fee" paid by card or JazzCash, a fake antivirus subscription that renews every year, or far worse — the scammer installs real malware while pretending to clean the computer, steals saved passwords and bank details, and sometimes locks the computer and demands a ransom to unlock it. The good news is that tech support scams are easy to spot once you know the pattern. Real companies never behave the way these scammers do. This guide explains how tech support scams actually work, the 10 red flags that give them away, what Microsoft, Google and Apple never do, how to escape a pop-up that locks your screen, exactly what to do if you already paid or gave someone remote access, how to report the scam in Pakistan, and how to protect your elderly parents and relatives — who are the scammers' favourite targets. Everything here is defensive: protecting yourself, your family, and your money from tech support fraud.

How tech support scams actually work

Tech support scams are not one trick — they are several tricks using the same disguise. These are the versions reported most often in Pakistan:

  • The scary pop-up warning (the most common). You click a link, an ad, or a download button on a website — sometimes even a normal-looking news or video site — and a full-screen warning appears. It uses the Microsoft or Windows logo, red colours, a fake error code, and a loud alarm sound. It says your computer is infected, your data will be deleted, and you must call a "support number" immediately. The pop-up is just a webpage. It cannot see your files, it cannot detect a virus, and it cannot lock your computer — it only looks like it can. Its only job is to frighten you into calling the number. Our fake website guide explains how these lookalike pages are built to fool you.
  • The cold call from "Microsoft" or "Google". A caller claims to be from Microsoft, Google, Apple, or your internet provider. They say your computer is sending error reports, your Windows licence has expired, or hackers are using your connection. They may know your name and city — information that is easy to buy or guess. They sound calm, professional, and helpful. Then they ask you to install a remote-access app — AnyDesk, TeamViewer, UltraViewer, or a similar tool — so they can "fix" your computer. Once you give them access, they can see everything on your screen, open your files, and install whatever they want. A real company never calls you out of the blue about your computer.
  • The fake support number in search results. Your printer stops working, or you forget your email password, so you search Google for "HP printer support" or "Gmail helpline". The top results are ads for fake support companies with toll-free numbers. You call, and a friendly "agent" charges you hundreds of rupees — or thousands — for a "fix" that is a five-minute job, or for nothing at all. Always find the official support number on the company's real website, never from a search ad.
  • The fake antivirus renewal call or email. You receive a call or an email saying your antivirus subscription — Norton, McAfee, or another brand — is about to renew for a large amount, and you must call to cancel it. When you call, the "agent" asks you to install remote-access software so they can "process the refund". Instead of a refund, they steal your bank details or install malware. Real antivirus companies never ask you to install remote-access software to cancel a subscription. Our SMS scams guide explains the same refund trick used in fake messages.
  • The "your account will be closed" message on WhatsApp or email. A message claims your Microsoft, Google, or bank account will be closed, or that a payment failed, and gives you a number to call or a link to click. The link opens a fake login page that steals your password, or the number connects you to a scammer who asks for your OTP and bank details. Our phishing protection guide covers exactly how these fake messages work.
  • The free "PC cleaner" or "speed booster" download. An ad promises to clean your computer, speed it up, or remove viruses for free. You download and install it, and it "finds" hundreds of serious problems — all fake. Then it demands payment to fix them. Worse, some of these fake cleaners are themselves malware that steal your data or show you even more scam pop-ups. Only download software from the official website of a company you trust.

10 red flags of a tech support scam

If you see even two or three of these, stop and verify before you call a number, click a link, install anything, or pay:

  • 1. They contacted you first. Microsoft, Google, and Apple never call you, email you, or send you a pop-up to tell you that your computer has a problem. If someone contacts you first about a "problem" with your device, it is a scam. Real companies wait for you to contact them.
  • 2. A pop-up or a voice tells you to call a number urgently. A real warning from Windows or your antivirus never tells you to call a phone number. It gives you a button inside your own security software — never a phone number on a webpage, and never a recorded voice shouting at you.
  • 3. They create panic and urgency. "Your files will be deleted in 5 minutes!", "Hackers are stealing your bank details right now!", "Do not turn off your computer!" Scammers rush you because a frightened person does not think clearly. A real support agent never pressures you to decide in seconds.
  • 4. They ask you to install remote-access software. AnyDesk, TeamViewer, UltraViewer, or any app that lets a stranger see and control your screen — a real support company only uses remote access after you contacted them yourself through their official website, and only with clear identification. A stranger who calls you first and asks for remote access is a criminal.
  • 5. They ask for your password, PIN, or OTP. No real support agent will ever ask for your password, your ATM PIN, or the one-time code from your phone. Anyone who asks is a criminal. Our password security guide explains how to build strong, unique passwords that only you know.
  • 6. They ask you to pay with gift cards, JazzCash, Easypaisa, or cryptocurrency. A real company charges your card through its official website or store. If anyone asks you to pay with gift cards, a mobile wallet transfer to a personal number, or crypto, the money is gone forever and cannot be traced. That is why scammers love these payment methods.
  • 7. The "technician" shows you fake proof. The scammer opens the Windows Event Viewer or a command window and points at red warnings and error messages, claiming they prove your computer is infected. In reality, every Windows computer shows warnings in the Event Viewer — they are normal system messages, not proof of a virus. The scammer is showing you normal parts of your own computer and calling them a disease.
  • 8. The price keeps growing. First it is a small "diagnosis fee", then a "repair fee", then a "lifetime protection plan", then a "security upgrade". Each payment is always the last one — until the next one. This is the classic advance-fee pattern. Stop paying. There was never a problem to fix.
  • 9. They tell you not to tell anyone. "Do not tell your family, do not call your bank, do not hang up." Scammers say this because the first person you told would warn you. A real support agent has no reason to keep your call secret.
  • 10. The contact details do not match the real company. The pop-up's phone number, the email address, or the website is not the one listed on the company's official website. The logo is blurry, the spelling is wrong, or the domain is almost — but not quite — the real one. Check the real company's website yourself and compare.

If several of these apply, treat the "support" as fake until proven otherwise — not the other way round. The burden of proof is on them, not on you.

Step 1: What Microsoft, Google and Apple never do

Memorise these rules. They end almost every tech support scam on the spot:

  • They never call you first about your computer. Microsoft, Google, and Apple do not make unsolicited calls about viruses, errors, licences, or hacked accounts. If you receive such a call, hang up. You can always call the company yourself on the number listed on its official website.
  • They never send pop-ups with a phone number to call. A real security warning from Windows or your antivirus appears inside your own security software, not as a full-screen webpage with an alarm sound and a phone number. A webpage cannot scan your computer — any webpage that claims it found a virus on your device is lying.
  • They never ask for your password, PIN, or OTP. No employee of any real company will ever ask for these. Not to "verify your identity", not to "process a refund", not for any reason at all.
  • They never ask you to install remote-access software out of the blue. Remote access is only used after you contacted official support yourself, through the official website, and only after your identity has been verified through official channels.
  • They never ask for payment in gift cards, mobile wallets, or cryptocurrency. Real companies charge through their official store or billing page. A request to pay a personal JazzCash or Easypaisa number, or to buy gift cards, is always a scam.
  • They never threaten you or rush you. "Your files will be deleted in five minutes" is not how a real company talks to its customers. Real support gives you time, reference numbers, and a written record of the conversation.

Step 2: A pop-up has locked your screen — what to do now

A scam pop-up can look terrifying: full screen, alarm sound, a warning that you must not turn off your computer. Stay calm. Your computer is not locked — only your browser window is stuck on a webpage. Here is exactly what to do, in order:

  1. Do not call the number. Do not click anything on the pop-up. Do not click "OK", "Scan now", "Fix now", or any button inside the warning. Do not enter your name, phone number, or card details anywhere on that page. The number connects you to the scammer; the buttons can download malware.
  2. Close the browser tab. Try pressing Ctrl + W to close the current tab, or Alt + F4 to close the browser window. On a Mac, press Command + W or Command + Q to quit the browser.
  3. If the browser will not close, force it to quit. On Windows, press Ctrl + Shift + Esc to open Task Manager, find your browser (Chrome, Edge, or Firefox) in the list, click it, and press "End task". On a Mac, press Command + Option + Esc, select your browser, and click "Force Quit". This closes the browser completely — the pop-up dies with it.
  4. Turn the sound off if the alarm keeps beeping. Press the mute key on your keyboard, or turn your speakers down. The sound is only there to panic you — it cannot harm your computer.
  5. Restart your browser — but do not restore the old tabs. When your browser asks "Restore pages?", click "No" or start a new session. Restoring the tabs brings the scam page back. If the pop-up returns, close the browser again and clear your browsing history before reopening it.
  6. Run a scan with your real antivirus. Open the antivirus you already have installed — Windows Defender, or a trusted brand you bought yourself — and run a full scan. Do this to be safe, even though the pop-up itself almost never installs anything if you did not click or download. Our malware removal guide explains the full cleaning steps if anything suspicious is found.
  7. Do not turn your computer off in a panic. The warning says "do not turn off your computer" only to keep you on the page. Turning it off will not delete your files, and it will not make things worse — but there is no need to turn it off at all. Closing the browser is enough.

That is all. A pop-up cannot infect your computer just by appearing on your screen. It only becomes dangerous if you call the number, click its buttons, or download what it offers.

Step 3: You already let them in or paid — act now

If you already installed remote-access software, let a stranger control your computer, or paid money, do not feel ashamed — these scams are designed by professionals to fool careful people, and they work because they use fear, not stupidity. Act fast, because the first hours matter most:

  1. Disconnect from the internet immediately. Unplug the network cable or turn off Wi-Fi. This cuts the scammer's connection to your computer the moment you do it. Do this first, before anything else.
  2. Uninstall the remote-access app. Open your installed programs list and remove AnyDesk, TeamViewer, UltraViewer, or whatever app they asked you to install. Then restart your computer. If you are not sure how, ask a trusted family member or a real local technician — not anyone who called you.
  3. Run a full scan with a trusted antivirus. Use Windows Defender or a well-known antivirus you installed yourself. Remove anything it finds. If the scammer installed something, a full scan is the fastest way to find it. Our malware removal guide gives the step-by-step cleaning process, and our guide to telling if your phone is hacked covers the warning signs on mobile too.
  4. Change your passwords — starting with your email and your bank. The scammer may have seen your saved passwords on screen. Change your email password first, then your online banking password, then your social media passwords. Use a different, strong password for each account. Turn on two-factor authentication everywhere you can. Our password security guide explains how to build strong, unique passwords.
  5. If you paid by card, call your bank immediately. Report the payment as fraud and ask whether the transaction can be reversed or your card blocked and replaced. The sooner you report, the better the chance of getting your money back. If you paid by JazzCash or Easypaisa, call their helpline and report the recipient's account as fraud.
  6. If you paid with gift cards, report the card numbers. Call the gift card company and report the card numbers as stolen. Sometimes the cards can be frozen if the scammer has not spent them yet — but act within hours, not days.
  7. If you shared bank details or your CNIC copy, watch for identity theft. Check your bank accounts and mobile wallets daily for transactions you did not make. Tell your bank that your details were shared and ask what protections they can add. Our identity theft protection guide explains the full steps, and our data breach recovery guide explains how to recover after your information is exposed.
  8. Keep all the evidence. Save screenshots of the pop-up, the phone number, the chat, the payment receipt, and the name of any app they asked you to install. Do not delete anything — you will need it for the report.
  9. Warn others. Tell your family and friends the scammer's number and what the pop-up looked like. One warning in your family group can save the next person.

How to report a tech support scam in Pakistan

Reporting helps the police and the platforms stop the same scammer from targeting the next person. Keep your screenshots and payment receipts ready before you report:

  • FIA Cybercrime Wing. Report tech support fraud to the FIA Cybercrime Wing — online through their complaint portal, or at your nearest FIA Cybercrime Reporting Centre. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and the evidence to keep.
  • The platform where you found the scam. Report the fake pop-up, the fake support ad, or the fake account on Google, Facebook, WhatsApp, or YouTube. Reporting gets the ad or page removed so it cannot reach more victims.
  • PTA, for scam calls and SMS. If the scammer called or messaged you, forward the SMS to 668 — the PTA's short code for reporting spam and scam messages — and report the caller's number.
  • Your bank or wallet provider. Report the recipient's JazzCash, Easypaisa, or bank account as a fraud account. Banks and wallet companies can flag and block accounts that receive fraud reports.
  • The real company, if its name was used. If a scammer pretended to be Microsoft, Google, Apple, or an antivirus brand, tell the real company through its official website. These companies collect scam reports and work to take the fake numbers and websites down.

Protecting elderly parents and family members

Tech support scammers target older people more than anyone else. Older relatives are more likely to trust a polite caller who says he is from Microsoft, more likely to panic at a red warning on the screen, and less likely to know that a real company never calls first. A short family conversation can prevent a large loss:

  • Teach them one golden rule: Microsoft never calls you. Repeat it until it sticks. If someone calls claiming to be from Microsoft, Google, or Apple, the correct action is always the same: hang up, and call a family member.
  • Make a family rule: never pay, never install, never share — without asking first. No payment, no app installation, and no password or OTP sharing without checking with a trusted family member first. One phone call to a son or daughter can stop a scam in seconds.
  • Set up their computer safely. Install a trusted antivirus yourself, turn on automatic updates, and install an ad-blocker in their browser — most scam pop-ups arrive through malicious ads. Remove any remote-access apps they do not need. Our family digital safety plan shows how to protect everyone at home together.
  • Show them what a scam pop-up looks like. Open a screenshot of a fake warning together and point out the signs: the phone number, the alarm, the red colours, the "do not turn off your computer" threat. Once they have seen one in a safe moment, the real one is far less frightening.
  • Put a note next to the computer. A simple handwritten card — "If a warning appears: do NOT call the number. Close the browser. Call me." — with your phone number on it. It sounds simple, but in a moment of panic, a written instruction is what people follow.
  • Check in regularly. Ask your parents or older relatives if they received any strange calls or pop-ups this week. Scammers often call the same person several times — catching the second call early can save everything.
  • Teach children the same rule. Children also see fake warnings while playing games or watching videos. Teach them never to call a number on the screen and always to tell an adult first. Our kids online safety guide covers this in detail.

Key Takeaways

  • Microsoft, Google and Apple never call you first about your computer — if someone contacts you first about a "problem", it is a scam.
  • A webpage cannot scan your computer — any pop-up that claims it found a virus and gives you a phone number is lying.
  • Never install remote-access software for a stranger — AnyDesk, TeamViewer, or any screen-sharing app handed to a caller gives them your whole computer.
  • Never share your password, PIN, or OTP with anyone — no real support agent will ever ask for these.
  • Never pay with gift cards, JazzCash, Easypaisa, or cryptocurrency to "fix" your computer — a real company never asks for these.
  • A locked screen is just a stuck browser tab — close the browser with Task Manager or Force Quit, do not restore the tabs, and run a scan.
  • If you already let them in, disconnect from the internet first — then uninstall the remote-access app, scan your computer, and change your passwords starting with your email and bank.
  • If you paid, call your bank or wallet immediately — the sooner you report, the better the chance of getting your money back.
  • Protect your elderly family members — teach them the golden rule, set up their computer safely, and make "call me first" the family rule.
  • Report every scam — to the FIA Cybercrime Wing, to PTA on 668, and to your bank, so the same scammer cannot target the next person.
