How to Spot Fake Parcel Delivery Scams (2026 Guide)

Last updated: 28 November 2026

By CyberSec Hub · 13 min read
Ethical Use Only: Only test systems you own or have written permission to test. Unauthorized hacking is illegal in Pakistan and worldwide. Everything here is for defensive security education and authorized testing only.

You ordered a phone cover, a pair of shoes, or a gift for your family, and you are waiting for the rider to call. Then a message arrives: "Your parcel is held at the warehouse. Pay Rs. 150 delivery fee now or it will be returned. Track here: bit.ly/xxxx." It looks urgent, it uses a courier name you know, and the amount is small — so you tap the link. That single tap is exactly what the scammer wanted. A fake parcel delivery scam is one of the fastest-growing online frauds in Pakistan, because almost everyone now orders online and almost everyone is waiting for a parcel at any given time. The scammer does not need to know what you ordered — the same message goes to thousands of numbers at once, and only a few people need to believe it. This guide explains how fake parcel scams work, the ten red flags that give them away, how to verify any delivery in five minutes, how cash-on-delivery scams and fake courier apps steal your money and data, and what to do if you already clicked, paid, or installed something. Everything here is defensive: protecting your money, your phone, and your personal information.

Why parcel delivery scams work so well

Most online scams work by creating fear or greed. Parcel scams work differently: they create expectation. Understanding why they succeed is your first line of defence:

  • Almost everyone is waiting for a parcel. Online shopping is now part of daily life in Pakistan — Daraz orders, Facebook sellers, and food deliveries. At any moment, millions of people are expecting a delivery, so a message about "your parcel" feels personal even when it was sent to ten thousand strangers. The scammer is playing a numbers game, and the odds are in their favour.
  • The amount is small, so you do not think twice. The "fee" is usually Rs. 100, Rs. 150, or Rs. 250 — a small "customs charge" or "re-delivery charge". It feels too small to be a scam, and too small to bother checking. But when thousands of people each pay Rs. 150, the scammer collects a fortune — and the real goal is often not the fee at all, but the bank card details, the OTP, or the app you install while "paying" it.
  • Curiosity does the scammer's work. "Your parcel could not be delivered" makes you want to know which parcel, from whom, and what went wrong. That curiosity makes you tap the link before you stop to think. A genuine courier message never needs you to be curious — it gives you the tracking number and the expected date, which you can check yourself.
  • Trust in courier names is easy to copy. TCS, Leopards Courier, M&P, Pakistan Post, and Daraz are names every Pakistani recognises and trusts. A scammer only needs to type one of those names into a message — or copy its logo onto a fake website — to borrow that trust. The message is not from the courier, but your brain reads the name and relaxes.
  • Cash on delivery makes the scam feel safe. Many people in Pakistan choose cash on delivery because it feels safer than paying online. Scammers know this, so they design a second version of the fraud around COD itself: a parcel arrives, you pay cash, and only when you open it do you discover it is empty, broken, or something you never ordered. Our safe online shopping guide explains how to check a seller before you order.

How fake parcel scams reach you

Parcel scammers use every channel where people expect delivery updates. Here are the most common ways they appear in Pakistan:

  • SMS messages — the most common route. A text from an ordinary mobile number says your parcel is held, delayed, or undeliverable, and gives you a short link to "track" or "reschedule" it. The link leads to a fake tracking page that asks for a small fee, your card details, or permission to install an app. Real couriers send SMS from their own registered sender IDs, and they do not ask you to pay a fee through a link in a text message. Our SMS scams guide explains how smishing works on every topic, not just parcels.
  • WhatsApp messages. A message from an unknown number, often with the logo of TCS or Leopards as its profile picture, says your parcel is waiting and asks you to confirm your address, CNIC number, or payment. Sometimes it arrives in a group, forwarded by someone you know, which makes it feel trustworthy. A real courier never asks for your CNIC or bank details on WhatsApp. Our WhatsApp security guide explains how hijacked accounts spread these messages.
  • Phone calls from "the courier". A caller claims to be a delivery rider or a courier helpline agent. They say your parcel is at the warehouse or a customs fee is due, and ask you to send money by JazzCash or Easypaisa, or to share the OTP that has just arrived on your phone. A real rider calls only to ask where your house is — never to ask for money, an OTP, or your bank details.
  • Fake tracking websites. A professional-looking website with a courier's name, logo, and colours asks you to enter a tracking number. Whatever number you type, it says the parcel is "held" and asks for a fee or your card details. The address is the giveaway: it is a slightly changed version of the real one, or a free domain ending like ".xyz", ".top", or ".site". Our guide to spotting fake websites shows how to check a web address before you trust it.
  • Never install a courier app from a link. Real courier apps come only from the Google Play Store — an APK from a message is almost always malware that steals your OTPs.
  • Fake sellers and marketplace parcels. You order a mobile phone from a Facebook or Instagram seller at a price that seems too good to be true. A parcel arrives, you pay cash on delivery, and inside is a stone, a broken old phone, or nothing at all. The seller's page disappears the same day. This is a parcel scam too — the delivery was real, but the seller was fake. Our guide to fake marketplace sellers shows how to check a seller before you pay.
  • "Customs fee" and "import duty" scams. A message claims your international parcel is held at customs and you must pay a duty immediately. Real customs duty is handled through official channels and paperwork — never through a random SMS link asking for a JazzCash transfer to a personal number.

The 10 red flags of a fake parcel message

Check any delivery message against this list before you tap, pay, or reply. One red flag is a reason to pause; two or three mean the message is almost certainly a scam:

  1. You are not expecting a parcel — or the courier does not match. The message says your parcel is with TCS, but your order was sent by Leopards. Or it says a parcel is waiting and you have not ordered anything at all. Scammers send the same message to thousands of people, so most recipients were never expecting a parcel. If the courier name does not match your order, stop.
  2. It asks for a fee to release, deliver, or reschedule the parcel. A "warehouse fee", "customs charge", or "re-delivery fee" paid through a link. Real couriers do not charge a fee by SMS link to release a parcel — delivery charges are agreed when you order.
  3. The link is shortened or the website address looks wrong. Links like bit.ly or tinyurl, or a web address with a spelling mistake, an extra word, or a strange ending (".xyz", ".top", ".site") instead of the courier's real website. A real courier's tracking link goes to its own official domain. If you cannot read the full address clearly, do not tap it.
  4. It asks you to install an app or an APK file. "Download our tracking app to see your parcel." A courier's real app is on the Google Play Store, installed by you. Any delivery message that asks you to install an app from a link is dangerous — the file is almost always malware designed to steal your OTPs and bank details.
  5. Extreme urgency — "today or it will be returned". "Pay within 2 hours or your parcel will be destroyed." "Last chance to claim your package." Real couriers hold parcels for days, attempt delivery more than once, and return them to the sender after a proper waiting period. A two-hour deadline is a pressure tactic, not a delivery policy.
  6. It asks for your OTP, PIN, CNIC number, or bank details. No courier, bank, or government department will ever ask for your OTP or ATM PIN — not on a call, not in a message, not on a form. A delivery message that asks for these is trying to take over your bank account or wallet. Our identity theft protection guide explains what criminals do with stolen personal information.
  7. Payment goes to a personal JazzCash, Easypaisa, or bank account. If the "fee" must be sent to a person's name and mobile number, that is the single biggest warning sign. A real courier collects payments in the company's own account, in the company's name.
  8. The sender is an ordinary mobile number. Real courier updates come from the courier's registered sender ID or official short code, and the message includes your tracking number and order details. A delivery message from an ordinary 03XX number, especially one with no tracking number you recognise, deserves a careful check.
  9. The message has no details you can check. No tracking number, no sender name, no order number, no expected delivery date — just "your parcel" and a link. A genuine delivery message is specific, because the courier knows exactly which parcel it is talking about.
  10. Bad language, wrong branding, or mismatched details. Spelling mistakes in the courier's name, a logo that looks slightly off, or a message that mixes two different courier names. Real companies proofread their messages. Scammers working fast, in bulk, usually do not.

How to verify a delivery in 5 minutes

Before you tap any delivery link, pay any fee, or reply to any courier message, spend five minutes on these checks. It is the difference between receiving your parcel and funding a scammer:

  • Check your own orders first. Open the app or website where you ordered and check whether a parcel is actually on its way, which courier is carrying it, and what the tracking number is. If nothing matches the message, the message is fake. Keep a simple list of parcels you are expecting, so a surprise "delivery" message stands out immediately.
  • Track the parcel on the courier's official website yourself. Do not tap the link in the message. Open your browser, type the courier's real web address yourself (or open its official app from the Play Store), and enter the tracking number there. The official tracking page will tell you the parcel's real status. If the tracking number does not exist on the official site, the message is a scam.
  • Call the courier on its official helpline. Find the helpline number on the courier's own website — not in the message — and call it. Give them the tracking number and ask about the parcel's status and whether any fee is due. A two-minute call settles the question completely, and a genuine courier will never mind you checking.
  • Search the number and the link. Type the sender's mobile number into Google, followed by "scam" or "fraud". If other people have received the same message, someone has usually posted a warning. Scammers often reuse the same fake tracking numbers for weeks.
  • Compare the fee with your order. If the message asks for a delivery fee, compare it with what you agreed at checkout. If you chose cash on delivery, the only amount you pay is the order total when the parcel is in your hands — not a separate "release fee" sent in advance.
  • Check the web address letter by letter. If you must open a tracking link, look at the address bar first. A real tracking page lives on the courier's own domain. One changed letter, an extra word, or a free domain ending means the page is fake — close it immediately and do not type anything into it.

Cash on delivery (COD) scams — how to protect yourself

Cash on delivery feels safe because you pay only when the parcel is in your hands. Scammers have adapted, and COD has its own traps:

  • The empty or wrong parcel. The most common COD scam: you pay for a phone or a branded item, open the box, and find a stone, a piece of soap, an old broken phone, or nothing at all. The seller disappears, and the courier is not responsible for what is inside the box. Prevention starts before you order: buy only from sellers with real reviews, a real page history, and a working contact number.
  • The sealed-parcel pressure. The rider says the parcel cannot be opened before payment — "company policy". That is true for most couriers, which is exactly why scammers rely on it: you must pay before you can see what you bought. Reduce the risk by checking the parcel before you pay: look at the weight, check the sender's name and address on the label, and compare the parcel's size with what you ordered. A phone should not feel like a stone, and shoes should not arrive in an envelope.
  • The parcel you never ordered. A parcel arrives for you, cash on delivery, but you never ordered anything. The rider insists you must pay. Do not pay for a parcel you did not order — refuse it and let the rider take it back. Scammers send unordered parcels hoping someone in the house will pay without checking, especially when the person who ordered is not at home. Tell everyone in your family: never pay for a parcel unless the person who ordered it has confirmed it.
  • The inflated price. The parcel label shows a different, higher amount than the price you agreed with the seller. Check the amount on the label against your order confirmation before you hand over any money. If the price is wrong, refuse the parcel and contact the seller.
  • Record the unboxing. For expensive orders, record a video while you open the parcel, showing the sealed box, the label, and the item inside. If the item is wrong or damaged, this video is your evidence for a refund claim with the seller or the marketplace. Keep the packaging, the label, and the payment receipt until you are sure the item is correct.

Fake courier apps and APK files — the most dangerous version

Of all the parcel scams, the fake app version causes the most damage, because it does not just steal a fee — it can take over your phone:

  • How it works. The message says you must install the courier's "tracking app" to see your parcel or reschedule delivery. The download link gives you an APK file — an Android app installed from outside the Play Store. That app asks for permissions: read your SMS, access your contacts, draw over other apps. Once installed, it can read the OTP codes your bank sends you and even make transfers from your mobile wallet while you sleep.
  • Never install an app from a delivery link. A real courier's app is on the Google Play Store, installed by you, from the store. No courier needs you to install an app to deliver a parcel — and no courier sends an APK file by SMS or WhatsApp. If a message asks you to install anything, delete the message.
  • Check the app before you install it. If you do need a courier's app, search for it in the Play Store yourself. Check the developer name, the number of downloads, and the reviews. A real courier app has a verified developer and a large number of downloads; a copycat has a similar name, a new developer account, and very few downloads.
  • If you already installed a suspicious app, act immediately. Uninstall it at once, then restart your phone. Change the passwords of your email, your bank app, and your JazzCash or Easypaisa account — from a different device if you can. Turn on two-factor authentication. Check your bank and wallet statements for transactions you did not make, and call your bank if you see anything strange. Our malware removal guide gives the full cleanup steps, and our guide to telling if your phone is hacked lists the warning signs to watch for afterwards.
  • Turn off "install from unknown sources" when you are done. If your phone ever asks you to allow installation from unknown sources, say no unless you are deliberately installing a trusted app yourself. Leaving that setting on makes the next fake app one tap away from installing.

Safe habits for online shopping and deliveries

A few habits make you almost immune to parcel scams, because the scammer's message will always contradict something you already know:

  • Order only from sellers you have checked. Before you order from a new Facebook, Instagram, or WhatsApp seller, look at the page's history: when it was created, how many real reviews it has, and whether customers post photos of what they received. A page created last week, with only product photos and no real customers, is a risk. Our fake website guide shows the same checks for online stores.
  • Save every order confirmation. Keep the order number, the agreed price, and the tracking number in one place — a notes app or a screenshot folder. When a delivery message arrives, you can compare it with the real order in seconds, instead of guessing from memory.
  • Use the courier's official app or website for tracking. Install the official apps of the couriers you use most, from the Play Store, and track your parcels there. When a message arrives, you already know where your parcel is — you do not need the message's link at all.
  • Never click a delivery link — type the address yourself. This one habit defeats most parcel scams. A link in a message can lead anywhere; an address you type yourself can only lead to the real website. The same rule applies to emails and WhatsApp messages.
  • Keep a list of parcels you are expecting. A short note — "Daraz shoes, TCS, Rs. 1,800, COD" — is enough. When a message talks about a parcel that is not on your list, you will spot the mismatch immediately, instead of wondering whether you forgot an order.
  • Do not pay any fee before the parcel is in your hands. Delivery charges are part of the order. Any genuine extra charge — and these are rare — is explained by the courier through its official channels, with a proper receipt, not demanded by SMS link. If someone wants money before delivery, it is not a delivery.

What to do if you already clicked, paid, or installed the app

If you tapped the link, paid the fee, or installed the app and now suspect it was a scam, act quickly — the sooner you act, the better your chances:

  • If you only tapped the link, do not type anything. Close the page immediately. Do not enter your name, address, card number, or any other detail. If you did enter your card details, call your bank at once and ask them to block the card. If you entered your email and password, change that password immediately, and change it everywhere else you used the same one.
  • If you paid a fee, contact your bank or wallet provider immediately. Call your bank, JazzCash, or Easypaisa helpline and explain that you sent money to a suspected fraud. Ask whether the transaction can be reversed or the recipient's account can be frozen. Reversals are not guaranteed, especially for wallet transfers, but they are sometimes possible if you report within hours. Do not wait until tomorrow.
  • If you installed an app, remove it and secure your accounts. Uninstall the app, restart your phone, and change the passwords of your email, bank, and wallet accounts from another device. Turn on two-factor authentication. Check your bank and wallet statements carefully for the next few days. If your phone behaves strangely — battery draining fast, apps opening by themselves, messages you did not send — read our malware removal guide and our data breach recovery guide.
  • If you shared an OTP or a PIN, call your bank immediately. An OTP or PIN in a scammer's hands can empty an account in minutes. Call your bank's helpline, explain what happened, and ask them to block your card and freeze online transactions. Change your mobile wallet PIN as well.
  • Save all the evidence. Take screenshots of the message, the link, the fake website, the payment confirmation, and the recipient's number or account details. Save the chat history. Do not delete anything — this evidence is what investigators need, and the scammer will delete their page and number as soon as people start reporting it.
  • Report it. Report the scam to the FIA Cybercrime Wing through its online complaint system, and to the PTA if it came by SMS or phone call. Report the number to your mobile network, and the page or account to Facebook, WhatsApp, or whichever platform carried it. Also tell the real courier — TCS, Leopards, or whichever name was misused — so it can warn other customers. Our guide to reporting cybercrime and online scams gives the exact steps and links.
  • Warn the people around you. Tell your family and friends about the exact message you received, so they recognise it when it arrives on their phones. Scammers send the same message to whole neighbourhoods — your warning can protect dozens of people.
  • Do not feel ashamed. Parcel scammers succeed because they exploit a normal, everyday situation — waiting for a delivery — not because their victims are foolish. Reporting it helps everyone, and staying silent only protects the scammer.

Key Takeaways

  • Fake parcel scams work because almost everyone is waiting for a delivery — the scammer sends the same message to thousands of people and only needs a few to believe it.
  • Never tap a delivery link in a message. Track your parcel on the courier's official website or app, by typing the address yourself.
  • No real courier charges a "release fee" or "customs fee" through an SMS link. Delivery charges are agreed when you order.
  • Never install a courier app from a link. Real courier apps come only from the Google Play Store — an APK from a message is almost always malware that steals your OTPs.
  • Check the 10 red flags: a courier that does not match your order, a fee demanded by link, a shortened or wrong web address, an app to install, extreme urgency, a request for your OTP or PIN, payment to a personal wallet, an ordinary mobile number as sender, no checkable details, and sloppy branding.
  • Verify any delivery in five minutes: check your own orders, track on the official site, call the official helpline, and search the sender's number.
  • For cash on delivery: check the label and the price before you pay, never pay for a parcel you did not order, and record the unboxing of expensive items.
  • If you already clicked, paid, or installed an app: call your bank or wallet provider immediately, uninstall the app, change your passwords from another device, save all the evidence, and report it to the FIA Cybercrime Wing and the PTA.
  • Never share your OTP, PIN, or card details with anyone, for any delivery. No real courier ever asks for them.
  • Everything here is defensive and legal — protecting your money, your phone, and your personal information from people who abuse the trust we place in deliveries.
